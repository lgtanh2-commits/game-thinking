Defining Culture

Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Salen & Zimmerman

"The game of Basketball was invented by Dr. James Naismith in 1891. In that year, in Springfield, Massachusetts, Naismith wrote out thirteen rules for a game using a ball and two peach baskets, nailed up ten feet off the ground. A hundred years later, a ball going through a hoop had become the symbol of an entire culture." — Dave Hickey, on Basketball as cultural object

Introducing Culture

The previous three units of Rules of Play explored games as systems of rules, as sites of play, and as experiences of meaning. Unit 4 — Culture — takes up a different set of questions: What happens when games enter the wider world? How do games reflect and transform the societies in which they are played?

Moving beyond the magic circle does not abandon everything established before. Instead, Culture builds on the formal, experiential, and social dimensions of games, adding the dimension of the cultural context that surrounds them. Games are never sealed off from the world. They are embedded in histories, ideologies, economies, and communities of meaning that shape how they are played, what they signify, and who plays them.

The key insight of this unit is that meaningful play is not only produced inside a game, but also in the friction between a game and the culture surrounding it. Understanding this friction is essential to understanding what games are and what they can do.

Culture: A Framework

The word "culture" is notoriously difficult to define. Anthropologist Edward Tylor offered one of the earliest formal definitions in 1871: culture is "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."

For game design purposes, a more operational definition proves useful. John Bodley identified eight approaches to understanding culture, each emphasizing a different facet of the concept:

TopicalA list of topics or categories composing culture: economics, religion, technology, family.
HistoricalCulture as social heritage — the accumulated traditions transmitted across generations.
BehavioralCulture as shared, learned human behavior: customs, rules, habits in action.
NormativeCulture as ideals, values, and rules held by a group — norms that guide behavior.
FunctionalCulture as the way humans solve problems and adapt to their environment.
MentalCulture as complex ideas in the minds of members — a cognitive map for navigating the world.
StructuralCulture as patterns of symbols, rituals, and meanings organized into systems.
SymbolicCulture as the shared arbitrary meanings attached to objects, events, and behaviors.

For game designers, the most productive starting point is the symbolic view: culture is the system of meanings shared by members of a society, the web of significance through which they interpret the world and each other. This definition captures what is most relevant to games — not culture as a list of artifacts, but culture as a system of meaning-making.

Culture and the Magic Circle

In the context of Rules of Play, culture can be defined operationally as everything that exists outside the magic circle of the game. This does not mean culture is irrelevant to what happens inside a game. On the contrary, the magic circle is never hermetically sealed. Cultural meanings seep in and out of the game in every direction. The rules, the players, the objects, and the spaces of a game all carry cultural freight before, during, and after play.

"Games exist within culture. Designers create games from within cultural contexts; players approach games from within cultural contexts; games are played, distributed, and reviewed within cultural contexts." — Salen & Zimmerman

Cultural Meanings: Three Examples

Dominos

Dominos originated in China, arrived in Europe in the 18th century, and today carries very different meanings in different cultural settings. In Cuban communities in Miami, dominos is a serious social institution, played in parks and cafes with elaborate rituals of shuffling, bidding, and trash talk. In academic game studies, it is studied as a combinatorial system. In children's play, it becomes a device for chain-reaction toppling. The same game — same tiles, same rules — carries entirely different cultural meanings depending on its context.

Scrabble

Scrabble draws on the cultural institution of language. Its tiles embed the frequencies of English letter use, making it both a measure and a product of English literacy culture. Competitive Scrabble players memorize two- and three-letter words that have no common use outside the game, developing a private vocabulary that exists only within the magic circle. At the same time, arguments about what counts as a "real word" — slang, archaic usage, foreign borrowings — turn every game into a cultural negotiation.

Basketball

Of all examples, Basketball most dramatically illustrates the transformation of a game into a cultural object. Naismith's thirteen rules produced a sport. A century of cultural accretion produced something more: a symbol of urban identity, a vehicle of racial politics, a global entertainment industry, a mode of self-expression so rich that Dave Hickey could write an essay treating a basketball game as a work of art.

Cultural Texts: Trafficking in Signs

Anthropologist Clifford Geertz described culture as "webs of significance" — systems of signs and meanings that humans have woven and in which they are suspended. Reading a culture means reading its signs, interpreting its symbols, understanding what its artifacts and practices mean to the people who use them.

Games can be read as cultural texts. Like novels or films, games make meaning through their formal structures. But unlike those media, games are interactive — players are not merely readers but participants in the production of meaning. This creates two distinct modes of reading:

Internal ReadingThe meanings produced within the magic circle by the game's own formal structures: rules, mechanics, representations, and player actions.
External ReadingThe meanings that flow in from outside the magic circle: cultural associations, social contexts, historical conditions, and ideological formations that players bring to the game.

Consider Ms. Pac-Man. Internally, it is a maze-chase game with formal rules about dots, ghosts, power pellets, and lives. Externally, it participates in a web of cultural meanings about gender (the "Ms." prefix, the bow, the lipstick), about femininity in the 1980s, about the market's response to the success of the original Pac-Man, about the representation of women in video game culture. Neither reading is more "correct" — both are necessary for a full understanding of the game as a cultural object.

"Every game exists within a cultural context that shapes its meaning, and in turn, games shape the cultural contexts they inhabit." — Salen & Zimmerman

Transformative Play, Revisited

The concept of transformative play — first introduced in the Play unit — takes on new significance in the context of culture. Transformative play is play in which the free movement of play alters the more rigid structures within which it occurs. When this alteration operates on the level of culture — when play transforms not just the state of a game but the meanings attached to a game within a culture — the result is culturally transformative play.

Basketball's transformation from peach-basket novelty to global cultural symbol is a case of culturally transformative play operating over a century. But the same process can occur in shorter loops: a mod that subverts a game's gender politics, a house rule that changes a game's social dynamics, a player community that transforms a competitive game into a site of social connection.

Locating Design

Designer and theorist Richard Buchanan defined design as "the activity of ordering, disordering, and reordering." This definition becomes especially apt when considered in a cultural frame. Game designers do not simply create systems in a vacuum. They order cultural materials — signs, values, narratives, social practices — into playable forms. By doing so, they inevitably take a position within culture, whether they intend to or not.

Recognizing the cultural embeddedness of game design is not a counsel of despair — it does not mean designers are prisoners of ideology who cannot make meaningful choices. On the contrary, it is an invitation to design with greater awareness: to understand the cultural materials you are working with, to anticipate the cultural meanings your game will produce, and to use those meanings deliberately in the service of meaningful play.

Key Concepts

  • Culture is the complex web of meanings, values, beliefs, and practices shared by a group — for game design purposes, everything outside the magic circle.
  • Bodley identified eight approaches: Topical, Historical, Behavioral, Normative, Functional, Mental, Structural, and Symbolic. The symbolic view — culture as shared meaning — is most useful for game analysis.
  • Games can be read as cultural texts, producing meaning through both internal (formal) and external (contextual) dimensions.
  • Culturally transformative play occurs when the free movement of play transforms not just game states but the cultural meanings surrounding a game.
  • Game design is the activity of ordering, disordering, and reordering cultural materials into playable form.
  • The magic circle is never hermetically sealed: cultural meanings continuously flow in and out of games, shaping and being shaped by play.
