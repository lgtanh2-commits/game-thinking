Rules of Play  ·  Unit Two: Rules  ·  Chapter 14

Games as Emergent Systems

How simple rules generate unpredictable complexity — and why this is the engine of meaningful play.

The Complexity of Tic-Tac-Toe

Tic-Tac-Toe feels like a trivial game. Most adults can play it to a draw every time after minimal experience. And yet the game is genuinely complex in a technical sense — a sense the authors are careful to define before using.

Complexity here does not mean "complicated." It refers to a specific property of systems: the existence of a large number of possible states and interactions that cannot be easily predicted or enumerated from the rules alone. Even Tic-Tac-Toe, the authors note, has 255,168 possible unique games (accounting for rotations and reflections). A player who has "solved" Tic-Tac-Toe has not enumerated all these possibilities — they have learned strategic heuristics that navigate the space efficiently. The complexity is real; humans simply develop shortcuts for managing it.

The point, made with reference to systems theorist John Campbell's concept of the complexity barrier, is that games almost always require crossing into genuine complexity to become interesting. A system that is too simple to cross the complexity barrier is either not a game or not a meaningful one.

The Messengers and Buildings Thought Experiment

To ground the concept of complexity, the authors offer a thought experiment about messengers and buildings. Imagine a city with buildings and messenger services. How complex is the system?

Two Buildings, Two Messengers The number of possible message-delivery paths is small and easily described. Every interaction can be enumerated. The system is simple — not in the pejorative sense, but in the technical sense: fully characterizable from its rules.
Ten Messengers, Fifty Buildings The number of possible delivery paths, routing decisions, timing interactions, and emergent traffic patterns becomes astronomically large. No one can enumerate them all. The system is complex — its behavior cannot be predicted from its rules alone.

The same principle applies to games. A game with very few elements and interactions may be simple in this technical sense — fully characterizable, with no surprises. A game that crosses the complexity threshold generates behavior that its rules alone do not predict. This is not a flaw; it is, in well-designed games, the source of everything interesting.

Simple Complexity

Complexity is not the exclusive property of complicated systems. Some of the most complex phenomena — in the technical sense — arise from extremely simple rules. The classic example is the three-body gravitational problem: even three massive bodies interacting gravitationally generate dynamics so complex that no closed-form solution exists. The rules are simple (Newtonian gravity); the behavior is irreducibly complex.

Even ordinary human conversation is complex in this sense. Two people talking follow simple turn-taking rules, but the space of possible conversations is infinite, and no conversation is fully predictable from the rules of conversation alone. The authors use this example to make the point that complexity is everywhere, not just in elaborate systems.

What Complexity Is Not

Physicist Per Bak clarifies what complexity means by saying what it is not. A system is complex when it sits in a particular zone between two failure modes:

Fully Ordered (Not Complex) A system where every element is in a fixed, predictable state. No surprises. No emergence. The system is completely characterized by its rules. Everything that will happen is determined and boring.
Fully Chaotic (Not Complex) A system where behavior is completely random and uncorrelated. No patterns. No structure. The system cannot be characterized at all — there is nothing to understand or exploit. Also boring, in a different way.

True complexity — and with it, the potential for meaningful play — exists in the zone between order and chaos: a system structured enough to generate patterns, but rich enough that those patterns are never fully predictable.

Four Kinds of Systems

Complexity scientist Christopher Langton identifies four broad categories that map onto this spectrum:

Fixed

All elements converge to a fixed, unchanging state. Complete predictability. No new behavior emerges over time.

Periodic

Elements oscillate in stable, repeating cycles. Structured, but cycles are predictable once identified.

Complex

Rich, varied, often surprising behavior. Patterns emerge but are never fully predictable. The zone of life, games, and interesting systems.

Chaotic

No coherent patterns. Behavior is essentially random. No useful information can be extracted about future states from current ones.

← Too ordered, too predictable Too random, no patterns →

Meaningful games occupy the complex zone. Fixed and periodic systems do not generate interesting play because players can fully predict outcomes. Chaotic systems do not generate meaningful play because player actions have no discernible effect on outcomes. The complex zone is where the complexity barrier is crossed and meaningful decisions become possible.

Two Horrible Games

To make this concrete, the authors describe two deliberately terrible game designs, each representing a failure mode:

Heads or Tails (Too Ordered) Players simultaneously reveal either heads or tails. If they match, player A wins; if not, player B wins. The outcome is entirely random, player choices have no effect on outcomes, and there is no skill, strategy, or meaningful decision. The game sits in the chaotic/random zone — there is no pattern for players to interact with.
The Grid (Too Chaotic) Players place tokens on a large grid. Points are scored based on an elaborate formula that incorporates positions of all tokens, turn count, prime number relationships, and several arbitrary modifiers. The scoring is incomprehensibly complex — not because it is rich, but because player actions and game outcomes are essentially uncorrelated. The system is chaotic in the technical sense: inputs do not produce predictable outputs.

Notice: both games are "complex" in the everyday sense — one involves probability, the other a complex formula. But neither is complex in Langton's sense. Heads or Tails is fixed/random (no patterns to exploit); The Grid is chaotic (patterns exist in the formula but are inaccessible to players). Neither produces meaningful play.

Variants That Cross the Complexity Barrier

The authors show how Heads or Tails can be modified to push it into the complex zone. Four variants, each crossing the barrier:

Decision Players must commit to heads or tails before seeing their opponent's choice — but are allowed to observe their opponent's previous choices and look for patterns. Now memory and prediction enter. Still simple, but has crossed into complexity.
Talking Players can communicate before revealing. Now negotiation, bluffing, promise-making, and social dynamics enter. A purely social layer has been added that generates emergent complexity.
Repeating Guess Each player secretly writes a sequence of choices before play begins. The sequences are compared turn by turn. Now pattern recognition, sequence strategy, and anticipation of the opponent's meta-strategy enter the game.
Increasing Risk Stakes escalate with each successive match — the longer you play, the more you risk. Now risk management, bankroll strategy, and exit-timing decisions enter. Pure chance becomes embedded in meaningful choice architecture.

Emergence

The concept of emergence is the heart of this chapter. Emergence refers to the appearance of complex, unpredictable behavior from the interaction of simple components following simple rules. Emergent behavior is not designed directly — it arises from the rules without being predicted by them.

Emergence is the arising of new, unexpected features and behaviors from the interaction of simpler components. The emergent properties are not present in any individual component — they appear in the system as a whole. — John Campbell, Grammatical Man

The key point for game design: if you design rules that produce emergence, you have designed a system that generates more game than you put in. The emergent behaviors — strategies, dynamics, player discoveries — are not in the rulebook. They arise from the interaction of rules with players and with each other.

Parts and the Whole: Holland's Analysis

Complexity theorist John Holland identifies what makes a system capable of generating emergence. Two properties are essential:

Coupled Interactions The parts of the system interact with each other — changes in one part affect other parts. In a game, changing the state of one element (moving a piece, spending a resource) affects the state of other elements. Without coupling, parts act independently and emergence is impossible.
Context-Dependent Interactions The effect of an action depends on the current state of the system — the same action in different contexts produces different results. In a game, moving a piece to square A may be brilliant in one board state and disastrous in another. Context-dependence is what makes strategy possible.

When these two properties combine, the whole becomes genuinely greater than the sum of its parts. The mathematical term is nonlinear: outputs cannot be predicted by simply summing inputs. The game state is not just the list of piece positions — it is the web of coupled, context-dependent relationships among them.

This is why game play is not the same as rule-following. Rules define the parts and their basic interactions. Emergence — the whole that is greater than the sum — is what game play actually consists of.

Life the Game: Conway's Game of Life

The most famous illustration of emergence from simple rules is mathematician John Conway's Game of Life. It is described as a "game" but is actually a zero-player cellular automaton — a system that evolves from an initial state according to fixed rules, with no player input.

The rules are four:

Birth
A dead cell with exactly three live neighbors becomes alive in the next generation.
Survival
A live cell with two or three live neighbors survives to the next generation.
Overcrowding
A live cell with four or more live neighbors dies from overcrowding.
Loneliness
A live cell with fewer than two live neighbors dies from isolation.

From these four rules, extraordinary emergent behaviors arise. The most celebrated is the glider: a five-cell pattern that moves diagonally across the grid, maintaining its form indefinitely. Nothing in the four rules predicts the glider — it is an emergent structure that arises from the interaction of rules with specific initial conditions.

More remarkable still is the glider gun: a pattern that generates an endless stream of gliders at regular intervals. The glider gun is even further removed from the rules than the glider itself — it is an emergent structure that generates emergent structures. Layers of emergence arise from four rules about birth and death.

Conway's Life demonstrates both Holland's properties perfectly:

Coupled Every cell's next state depends on its neighbors. Changes propagate. The grid is a single coupled system, not a collection of independent cells.
Context-Dependent The same local pattern (three live neighbors) produces different results depending on whether the central cell is alive or dead. Context determines outcome at every level.

Emergence in Games

Emergent behavior in games is not a curiosity — it is the primary mechanism by which games generate ongoing interest and novelty. The authors offer several examples:

Bluffing in Poker

The rules of Poker say nothing about bluffing. There is no "bluff" move defined in the ruleset. Yet bluffing is perhaps the most important strategic concept in Poker — a fully emergent behavior that arises from the interaction of hidden information, betting mechanics, and psychological dynamics among players. Bluffing in Poker is, the authors suggest, the game-design equivalent of the glider gun in Conway's Life: an emergent structure that generates emergent behaviors.

Tic-Tac-Toe's Hidden Complexity

Holland calculates that Tic-Tac-Toe, despite its simple rules, has 255,168 distinct possible games. A player who can reliably play to a draw has not mastered a simple system — they have learned to navigate a genuinely complex space through heuristics. The complexity is real; the game is simply small enough that humans can develop heuristics to manage it completely.

Go vs. Chess: The Complexity Paradox

Chess has more complex rules than Go. Go's rules can be stated in a single paragraph; Chess requires several pages. Yet Go is vastly more complex in the emergent sense — the space of possible games is inconceivably larger, the strategies are richer, and top-level play has remained beyond computer mastery far longer than Chess.

This apparent paradox resolves through the emergent lens. Rule complexity and emergent complexity are not the same thing. Go's simple rules produce more coupling and context-dependence — more emergent richness — than Chess's more complex rules. Simple rules can generate more emergence than complex ones.

Designing Surprise: Second-Order Design

The emergence framework transforms what it means to design a game. If emergence is real — if the interesting behavior of a game arises from rule interactions rather than from rules directly — then what does a designer actually do?

Discovering Through Play

The authors describe Populous, Will Wright's god-game, as an example of emergence discovered during design. The game includes both preachers (who convert followers peacefully) and warriors (who fight). The designers expected warfare to dominate, since it is faster. But in practice, preacher strategies frequently outperformed military ones — an emergent discovery that players made through play and that the designers had not anticipated. The preacher strategy was always latent in the rules; it took play to reveal it.

Game designer Marc LeBlanc describes this as game tuning — the process of adjusting rule parameters after emergent play reveals unexpected dynamics. The designer cannot fully predict what will emerge; they can only set up conditions, observe what arises, and tune accordingly.

Gearheads: Engines That Discover Each Other

The toy game Gearheads further illustrates emergence. Players wind up mechanical toy characters and release them; the characters interact according to their physical mechanics. Three emergent behaviors — which the designers could not have predicted from individual toy mechanics:

Punching Roaches One character type, when combined with another of a specific type, reliably knocks it backward — an interaction not designed into either toy individually.
Bomb Shield Certain character configurations protect others from a "bomb" character's area effect — emergent defensive strategy from physical toy interactions.
Perpetual Motion Specific combinations of characters enter self-sustaining cycles of motion — effectively a Gearheads equivalent of the glider in Conway's Life.

These behaviors were discovered through play, not designed directly. The designers created the conditions for emergence; players (and the toys themselves, through physical interaction) discovered what emerged.

Second-Order Design

These examples point toward the most important concept in the chapter: second-order design. A game designer does not directly design the experience of play. A game designer designs the rules that generate play. The experience is an emergent product of the rules meeting players — a second-order consequence of what the designer actually made.

When you design a game, you are not designing the experience of playing it. You are designing the formal system — the rules — that produces the experience when players engage with it. The experience is always an emergent product of that system and those players.

The best illustration is Magic: The Gathering. The designers of Magic created approximately 10,000 unique cards, each with specific effects defined by rules. The interactions between cards — the combos, counter-strategies, deck archetypes, and metagames — were not individually designed. They emerged from the rule interactions. The designers developed what the authors call a "sixth sense for rule ramifications" — an intuition for how rules would interact and what would emerge from their combinations.

This is second-order design: designing the system with an eye toward what the system will produce, rather than designing the output directly. It requires a different kind of thinking — not "what do I want players to experience?" but "what rules will produce the experience I want when players engage with them?"

Chapter Summary

  • Complexity in the technical sense refers to systems whose behavior cannot be predicted from their rules alone. Even Tic-Tac-Toe is complex by this definition (255,168 possible games). Games must cross the complexity barrier to support meaningful play.
  • Four system types (Langton): Fixed, Periodic, Complex, Chaotic. Meaningful games occupy the complex zone — structured enough for patterns, rich enough that those patterns are never fully predictable.
  • Emergence is the appearance of unpredictable, surprising behavior from simple interacting rules. Emergence is not designed directly; it arises from the rules meeting players in play.
  • Holland's two properties that enable emergence: coupled interactions (parts affect each other) and context-dependent interactions (the same action produces different results in different states). When these combine, the whole exceeds the sum of its parts.
  • Conway's Game of Life demonstrates emergence from four rules: gliders and glider guns arise from birth/survival/death conditions that contain no hint of self-propelling structures.
  • Bluffing in Poker and the complexity paradox of Go vs. Chess illustrate how emergent richness is independent of rule complexity. Simple rules (Go) can produce more emergence than complex rules (Chess).
  • Second-order design: game designers design rules, not experiences. The experience is an emergent product of rules meeting players. Designing well means developing intuition for what will emerge — not controlling emergence directly.
  • Discovery through play — Populous preachers, Gearheads engines — is a normal part of the design process because designers cannot fully predict what will emerge from their own rules.
