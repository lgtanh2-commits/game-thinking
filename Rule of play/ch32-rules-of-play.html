Games as Cultural Resistance

Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Salen & Zimmerman

Introducing Cultural Resistance

Games as Open Culture asked what forms of player-production occur in games and where they are situated relative to the magic circle. This chapter carries those questions further: how are forms of player-production designed, and why do they occur? What makes a community of cheaters and vigilantes emerge in SiSSYFiGHT 2000? What makes a programmer secretly modify SimCopter to subvert its gender politics?

The schema Games as Cultural Resistance focuses on design interventions that call specific attention to the borders of the magic circle through acts of creative resistance. "Resistance" here does not mean political opposition exclusively. It refers to a broader concept: friction — the tension that occurs when two phenomena come into conflictual contact. When games and their cultural contexts come into contact, when one acts to resist the other, the result is cultural resistance.

Framed this way, resistance is not necessarily negative. Play itself is already a form of resistance — defined in this book as "free movement within a more rigid structure," the free play of games already involves friction with the rule systems that contain it. The same logic operates at the cultural level: cultural contexts resist games, and games resist cultural contexts, producing friction that shapes the experience of play in deeply meaningful ways.

DIY Gaming: From Punk to DOOM

Rock critic Greil Marcus described the Sex Pistols' 1977 emergence as "a breach in the screen of received cultural assumptions governing what one expected to hear and how one expected to respond." Decades later, DOOM's infiltration of mainstream gaming culture produced a comparable breach — not through noise and outrage, but through a radically different model of cultural production.

The connection between punk rock and DOOM is not aesthetic. DOOM's horror-movie pulp kitsch is very different from punk's noisy anti-aesthetic. The link is ideological: both embodied a DIY (Do-It-Yourself) mentality — the belief that control of the tools of production could be wrested from the dominant culture and used to create an alternate version of reality. Playing in a punk band meant picking up a guitar and making noise; making a DOOM level meant learning a map editor and making monsters.

By releasing DOOM's source code and development tools to players, id Software made a breach in the assumptions of who gets to make games. The "free play" of DOOM's open source code made possible a grassroots ecology of player-created content that extended the game's life for decades — while simultaneously challenging deeply held assumptions about the division between player and designer.

Resistant Strategies: The Three Categories

Designed strategies of resistance in games — whether created by players or designers — are called game modifications, or mods. Modifications can be officially sanctioned (developer-distributed updates that fix bugs or add features) or unofficial (unsanctioned projects created and distributed by players). They call attention to the borders of the magic circle by creating friction between existing and alternative versions of the game.

Game modifications fall into three overlapping categories, each representing a different strategy for achieving cultural resistance:

Alteration Making changes to existing game structures — replacing representations, swapping characters, redesigning visual elements.
Juxtaposition Combining unexpected elements within a game space — placing culturally incongruous objects, characters, or settings together.
Reinvention Reworking entire game structures on deeper levels — transforming core mechanics, systems, or the fundamental premise of the game.

Strategies of Alteration

Alterations are modifications that rework existing forms of representation or interaction without fundamentally changing the game's core mechanics.

SOD (Jodi)

Artists Dirk Paesmans and Joan Heemskerk — known collectively as Jodi — created SOD, a modification of Castle Wolfenstein that strips the 3D shooter of all color and depth. Basic geometric forms — squares, circles, and triangles — replace the game's original visual components: the castle walls, guards, and weapons. Rather than replacing character sprites with alternative characters, Jodi designed resistance by completely emptying the game of figurative representation.

The resulting game space is rendered as simple geometric blocks and lines. Players are forced to consider how the game engine creates a meaningful space through illusions of depth, movement, and object behavior. Although the graphics code was altered, the soundtrack remained untouched — screaming triangles and barking, rotating squares that confront the player through absurdist juxtapositions.

Sailor Moon Wad

Developed in support of a "Save Our Sailors" (SOS) campaign to keep Sailor Moon on the air in North America, the Sailor Moon Wad alters DOOM's gothic architecture by littering the space with objects from the world of Sailor Moon: cupcakes, chocolate cake, hearts, roses, bunny suitcases, and messenger bees. The implicit masculinity of DOOM's aesthetic is playfully called into question through the addition of an entire system of feminized artifacts.

SimCopter Hack

The SimCopter Hack was the result of a collaboration between the art collective rtmark and a programmer working on the computer game SimCopter. While the game was still in development, the programmer substituted what rtmark termed "boy bimbos" for the game's token bikini-clad females, "infecting the game's traditional heterosexual gaze with homoerotic undertones." Maxis, the developer, discovered the modification after an estimated 80,000 copies had already been sold. The game may have been intended as economic sabotage; it also functioned as an elegant critique of normalized representations of gender in mainstream game design.

Strategies of Juxtaposition

Juxtaposition achieves resistance by placing unexpected elements together in the same game space — drawing on the long tradition of using unlikely combinations to "talk back" to culture, from Duchamp's readymades to punk safety-pin aesthetics.

Frag Queens

Many early female skins designed for Quake evolved out of a constraint of the software: the original game included only male 3D geometry for designing new skins. Players interested in playing female characters ended up with female skins mapped onto standard male muscular figures. The Psycho Men Slayers, a Quake clan, embraced this limitation, creating "frag queens" — avatars of deeply ambiguous gender that mixed feminine attributes (hair, clothing, facial features) with the male base figure. Not quite male, not quite female, the avatars challenged traditional notions of gender identity within gamer culture.

Blacklash

Designed by the London-based activist group Mongrel, Blacklash juxtaposes racial politics with arcade game nostalgia. A racial re-versioning of the arcade classic Tempest, Blacklash transforms the game's aliens into comic book renditions of hooded Ku Klux Klan members and uniformed British police officers — "a wake-up call for young black youth under threat by ignorance and racist fools." By using Tempest's shoot-or-be-shot mechanics as the basis for political commentary, Blacklash creates resistance between the formal and experiential components of the game, encouraging players to connect feelings of nostalgia for a beloved game with patterns of institutional racism.

Los Disneys

A patch for Marathon Infinity by Jason Huddy, Los Disneys transforms the game into a post-apocalyptic playground set in Disney's Magic Kingdom. Players can shoot oversized Disney characters wandering the grounds and blast their way through lengthy lines of children waiting for rides. Los Disneys creates friction between the simulated actions of the game and the external events those actions mirror. The substitution of aliens with guns by tourists with surveillance cameras offers a softer, more insidious experience of game attack and conflict — playing on the "play" of violence while simultaneously interrogating the ideological space of the theme park.

Strategies of Reinvention

Reinvention modifies the core structures of a game from the inside out — reshaping them at the deepest level, creating perhaps the most radical form of cultural resistance through transformative play.

Universal Square

In artist Uri Tzaig's Universal Square, two Soccer teams — one composed of Jewish Israelis, the other Arab Israelis — were invited to take part in a televised match in Lod, Israel in 1996. The game was played according to standard Soccer rules, with one modification: Tzaig redesigned the game to be played with not one ball, but two simultaneously.

The elegant design choice created resistance in multiple ways. The game could not be played in a "normal" fashion; players had to invent new forms of interaction that took into account the loss of a single unifying object. Spectators, whose eyes normally follow a single ball, looked in many different directions at once. The centralized power structures of the game were dislocated. In Tzaig's cultural context, the doubled Soccer game became a representation of the chaotic, decentered, and multi-layered cultural conflicts of the Middle East — and simultaneously a model for how such a complex conflict could be contained and resolved through new forms of interaction.

Counter-Strike

Counter-Strike, a modification of Half-Life, transformed the original game on numerous levels, creating a game experience wholly its own. The design shifted the representational plane entirely — stripping away Half-Life's science fiction narrative and replacing it with a naturalistic terrorism setting. As game critic Justin Hall wrote, "Counter-Strike exists only in the world prepared for it by the war in Vietnam and COPS, where game-players grew up seeing footage of real men with real guns."

Counter-Strike's reinvention was not only aesthetic and representational. The mod evolved out of a collaboration between small groups of shareware developers and later became a commercial product sold in stores, winning several major awards at the 2001 Game Developers' Conference. Its movement from noncommercial shareware culture to the commercial marketplace offers reinvention as a model for independent game development.

Friction on the Playground: SiSSYFiGHT

The SiSSYFiGHT 2000 cheating manifesto — excerpted at the chapter's opening — illustrates the complexities of resistance operating simultaneously at multiple levels. In this online game, cheaters resisted the authority of the game through unauthorized coordination. In response, vigilante players resisted the cheaters through community organization. The resulting manifesto was itself a form of player-production — a cultural artifact that entered the game from outside and eventually transformed its formal rules.

Word.com, the game's developer, responded to complaints about cheating by posting a "code of ethics" on the game website that formally banned private chat as a rule infraction. So-called cheaters bending the rules led to this structural transformation — a process in which cheating players, vigilante players, and game designers all took part. The magic circle contracted; a previously informal norm became an operational rule. Resistance in a game often leads to more resistance, creating multi-layered systems of cultural play.

"Games intrinsically support and encourage the movement of free play. It is always possible for players to throw a monkey wrench into the system, bending it into a new shape. Game designers must consider how this process can be enhanced or interrupted, modified or transformed. Fight the establishment! Down with the old rules! Vive la resistance!" — Salen & Zimmerman

Key Concepts

  • Games as Cultural Resistance focuses on the friction that occurs when games and their cultural contexts come into conflictual contact. This friction makes the normally invisible edges of the magic circle visible.
  • A DIY (Do-It-Yourself) approach to cultural resistance appears in game modifications — designed strategies of resistance that can be official (developer-sanctioned) or unofficial (player-created).
  • Three overlapping categories of game modification: Alteration (changing representational or interactive structures), Juxtaposition (placing unexpected elements together), and Reinvention (restructuring the game at a deeper level).
  • Alteration examples: SOD (Jodi), Sailor Moon Wad, SimCopter Hack — each challenging the representational assumptions of the original game.
  • Juxtaposition examples: Frag Queens, Blacklash, Los Disneys — each producing resistance through the collision of incongruous cultural elements.
  • Reinvention examples: Universal Square (Uri Tzaig's two-ball Soccer), Counter-Strike — each reworking the core structure of a game to create new cultural meanings.
  • Resistance flows naturally from the intrinsic qualities of games: free play always already involves friction with the rule structures that contain it.
