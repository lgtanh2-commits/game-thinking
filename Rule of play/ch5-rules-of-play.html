Chapter 5 Systems

The system is not the collection of things—it is the collection of interactions. The things are necessary but not sufficient. — Stephen W. Littlejohn, Theories of Human Communication

Introducing Systems

Games are systemic. But what exactly is a system? The dictionary offers eight distinct senses of the word — a "regularly interacting or interdependent group of items forming a unified whole," a "set of principles or procedures according to which something is done," an "organized scheme or method." What unites them all is the idea of organized complexity: things that stand in relation to one another, where the whole exceeds the sum of its parts.

Salen and Zimmerman use the concept of system as a formal analytical frame. Every game, they argue, can be understood as a system — or rather, as several interlocking systems simultaneously. Understanding how systems work is the prerequisite to understanding how games produce meaning, how designers shape experience, and how players interact with designed structures.

Elements of a System

Littlejohn identifies four elements that any system contains. Together these four form a minimal grammar for describing any game as a system.

Objects The parts, elements, or variables within the system. Objects can be physical, abstract, or dynamic — pieces, rules, players, scores, cards, time limits.
Attributes The qualities or properties of the system and its objects. A chess piece has attributes (color, shape, movement range); the game has attributes (turn-based structure, perfect information).
Internal Relationships The relations among the objects in the system. The knight's movement rule relates it to the board's geometry; the check rule relates king and attacking pieces. Relationships define the system's structure.
Environment The context surrounding the system. The environment is what the system is embedded in — physical space, cultural context, other systems, the players themselves.

Three Ways to Frame a System

Any game — Salen and Zimmerman use chess as their touchstone — can be viewed through three distinct systemic frames. These framings are not mutually exclusive; they are complementary lenses that illuminate different aspects of the same game.

Formal System

Chess as pieces and rules. 32 pieces, 64 squares, movement constraints, win condition. The formal system is the mathematical object the designer constructs.

Experiential System

Chess as two people playing. Tension, attention, time pressure, blunders and brilliance. The experiential system includes players as components — it is the game as it is actually lived.

Cultural System

Chess embedded in history, metaphor, and social meaning. The cold-war chess match, the chess prodigy, the chess metaphor for military strategy. The cultural system is the game as it circulates in the world.

These three framings — formal, experiential, cultural — will reappear throughout Rules of Play as the book's three primary schemas: Rules, Play, and Culture. They are not merely three ways to describe chess; they are three ways to describe every game.

Systems Within Systems

The three framings are not parallel — they are hierarchically embedded. The formal system sits at the center, enclosed by the experiential system, which is in turn enclosed by the cultural system. Each outer frame contains and gives meaning to the inner one.

Formal rules only become play when enacted by people — the formal system is embedded within the experiential. And the experience of playing is always already shot through with cultural meaning — the experiential system is embedded within the cultural. A designer who only attends to the formal system misses how the game will actually be lived and understood.

A Brief History of Systems Thinking

Systems thinking as a formal discipline has a traceable history. Ludwig von Bertalanffy proposed General Systems Theory in a 1928 thesis, arguing that the principles governing biological organisms were not reducible to physics and chemistry — life required a new science of organized wholes. By 1969 his General System Theory was published, laying out a framework applicable across disciplines.

Concurrent developments reinforced the field. Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (the science of regulation and feedback in machines and animals) emerged from World War II-era research. Complexity theory, emerging from physics and biology in the 1970s and 80s, extended the analysis of emergent behavior in large-scale systems. Together these currents established that organized complexity — systems — demanded its own conceptual tools, tools that game design now inherits.

Open and Closed Systems

Not all systems relate to their environments in the same way. Systems theorists distinguish between open and closed systems — a distinction that maps directly onto the three framings of games.

Closed System

A system that does not exchange energy or information with its environment. The formal system of a game is closed: the rules of chess are the rules of chess regardless of who plays or where or when.

Open System

A system that exchanges energy or information with its environment. The cultural system of a game is open: chess absorbs and radiates meaning as it moves through history and society.

The experiential system occupies a middle position — it can be treated as either open or closed depending on the analytical purpose. When studying the phenomenology of play in isolation, we may treat it as closed; when studying how player psychology, cultural expectations, and social dynamics shape the experience, we treat it as open. The flexibility of the framing is a feature, not a defect.

Summary

Chapter 5 establishes the systemic vocabulary that Rules of Play will use throughout. Any game can be described using four elements — objects, attributes, internal relationships, and environment — and can be framed from three complementary perspectives: as a formal system (the rules), as an experiential system (the play), and as a cultural system (the meaning). These three framings are nested: formal within experiential, experiential within cultural.

The historical context grounds systems thinking in a recognizable intellectual tradition — von Bertalanffy, cybernetics, complexity — and the open/closed distinction gives designers a practical handle on how games relate to the world outside them. With this systemic vocabulary in place, the next chapter turns to the concept of interactivity: how players engage with a system to produce meaningful choices.
