Games as Information Theory Systems

Information is not meaning — it is a measure of uncertainty. And uncertainty is the lifeblood of every game.

The random element is called entropy, the agent of chaos, which tends to mix up the unmixed, to destroy meaning. The nonrandom element is information, which exploits the uncertainty inherent in the entropy principle to generate new structures, to inform the world in novel ways. — Jeremy Campbell, Grammatical Man

Introducing "Information"

The word "information" is used in many different contexts in many different ways. We receive safety information on airplanes, earn degrees in information science, and suffer from information overload. The term is feisty to pin down.

Within the following two chapters, the book examines two different ways of understanding information as it relates formally to games. This chapter — Games as Information Theory Systems — frames games within the context of information theory, a close cousin of systems theory. The next chapter shifts to a commonsense understanding of information as knowledge.

Information theory studies the movement of information in a system. How does information flow within a game? How are choices made meaningful through the input and output of information? How do redundancy and uncertainty affect meaningful play from a formal perspective? What are the qualities of a game as a communication system?

Information Theory

The field of information theory arose after World War II in tangent with the emerging telecommunications industry. Since its formalization in the classic 1949 text by Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver, The Mathematical Theory of Communication, information theory has grown beyond its origins in electronics and cryptography to find applications in behavioral and social sciences.

Before going further, it is crucial to understand what information theory actually means by the word "information." Information theory does not use the word in the same way it is used in casual conversation. When you say you need more "information" about a car before purchasing it, you mean knowledge — more data so you can better understand the car. This is the opposite of how the word is used in information theory.

The word information, in this theory, is used in a special sense that must not be confused with its ordinary usage. In particular, information must not be confused with meaning. In fact, two messages, one of which is heavily loaded with meaning and the other of which is pure nonsense, can be exactly equivalent, from the present viewpoint, as regards information. — Warren Weaver, The Mathematical Theory of Communication

Unlike a knowledge-based understanding of the word, in information theory "information" is divorced from meaning. Stephen W. Littlejohn, a communications theorist, captures this directly:

Information is the measure of uncertainty in a situation. When a situation is completely predictable, no information is present. As used by the information theorist, the concept of information does not refer to a message, facts, or meaning. It is a concept bound only to the quantification of stimuli or signals in a situation. — Stephen W. Littlejohn, Theories of Human Communication

Information theory proceeds from two foundational premises, both evident in these quotes:

Meaning Is Irrelevant
Information has nothing to do with the content or meaning of a message. A string of coherent sentences and a string of pure nonsense can contain the same amount of information. Information theory concerns signal transmission — the formal structure of a message — not its content.
Information Measures Uncertainty
Information does not measure the "amount of stuff" in a message. It measures uncertainty. When a situation is completely predictable, no information is present. The more uncertain the outcome, the higher the informational content of whatever resolves it.

The J.L. Thought Experiment

To make information-as-uncertainty concrete, consider a thought experiment. Imagine a stranger named J.L. If you are trying to guess whether J.L. had eaten breakfast today, there are only two possible answers: "yes" or "no." You have a fair degree of certainty about what J.L. might answer — and because of this, the amount of information in the answer is low.

If you are trying to guess J.L.'s favorite color, you are still guessing a single word, but there are many more possibilities. The amount of information in the correct answer is higher, because you are selecting J.L.'s favorite color from a larger set of possible answers.

If you try to guess J.L.'s favorite word from all the words in the English language, the amount of information contained in the answer is higher still — there is so much uncertainty about what it might be. As Weaver puts it, information "relates not so much to what you do say, as to what you could say."

The key insight: a correct guess about J.L.'s favorite word contains the most information, because it also reduces the highest degree of uncertainty. Information and uncertainty travel together. More freedom in what might be sent as a message means more information in the message itself.

This connects directly to games. Games are contexts which provide players with the ability to make meaningful decisions. The information in a communication system is analogous to the space of possibility in a game. A player in a game with a large space of possibility is selecting an action from among many meaningful options — just as a high-information message is selected from among many possible signals.

Probability and Guesswork

Information, uncertainty, and probability all share a set of formal relationships. Think about a die roll as an information theory signal. If one die is rolled, there is less information — and less uncertainty — than if two dice are rolled, where the set of possible outcomes is greater. Two die rolls equal more possibilities, more uncertainty, and therefore more information.

Because guessing games rely on uncertainty, they make good information theory case studies. Consider Mastermind. Each turn, one player attempts to guess a coded sequence; her opponent tells her how correct or incorrect her guess was. The goal is to arrive at the correct answer within a limited number of turns. As an information theory system, Mastermind puts information at play: with each guess, the guesser narrows down the possible answers, decreasing uncertainty, carving out a single guess from a range of all possible guesses.

Twenty Questions works similarly. One player holds a piece of information — the answer — and the other player tries to guess that piece of information through yes or no questions. The vast realm of possible solutions can be reduced quickly and efficiently through a series of yes/no guesses — usually in fewer than twenty. The linguistic and cultural content of the answer is irrelevant from an information theory standpoint; what matters is the mathematical interrelationship between every possible Twenty Questions query and every possible yes or no answer.

Noise in the Channel

As information theory systems, games are rife with signal transmission and reception. Players communicate with each other through speech, written words, and gesture. They also communicate with the system of the game itself. During a turn in Monopoly, players roll the dice and receive the visual signal of the black dots on the die cubes, interpreting it as a numerical value. Signals manifest as speech between players, as written text on cards, and as visual information.

The one element in Shannon and Weaver's model not yet mentioned is noise — interference in the signal that enters into a channel of transmission. Noise increases the uncertainty of the signal, and therefore the amount of information it contains. In most classical engineering applications, noise is something to minimize: clear telephone conversations, television free of static and snow.

If games are framed as a system of information, however, noise is not necessarily negative. Sometimes entire games are premised on putting noise into a channel of communication. Consider the informal play activity of Telephone, in which people whisper a message around a circle. The pleasure for the participants comes precisely from the noise in the system. The original message receives noise as it passes from person to person, transforming the message a little bit each time. The signal that emerges from the long chain of whisperers undergoes a radical transformation — and that is exactly the fun of the game.

Similarly, Charades is based on the transmission of signals from one form to another. One player takes a written signal and transforms it into a set of improvised gestures, attempting to convey the information back to teammates while narrowing the uncertainty of the answer. In Telephone, pleasure emerges from the addition of noise. In Charades, the players fight against the noise inherent in the structure of the game. The pantomime gestures introduce noise through their inherent ambiguity. But it is this struggle against noise and uncertainty that makes Charades fun to play. The instant the correct answer rings out — the signal coming full circuit — is a deeply satisfying moment of play.

The phenomenon of noise as a desirable component of a game system is a function of the lusory attitude — the state of mind that players take on in order to enter the magic circle and play a game, the shared attitude that accepts the paradoxically "inefficient" means for achieving the goals of a game.

Redundancy in the System

Whereas noise negatively impacts the successful transmission of a signal, information theory also identifies its opposite — the counterbalance to noise known as redundancy. Redundancy refers to the fact that in information systems, a message can be successfully transmitted even if some of the signal is lost, because redundant information patterns compensate for "holes" in the data of the signal.

In most communication systems, such as spoken language, much more information is transmitted in a single statement than is minimally required to convey the signal. As Weaver notes, the redundancy of English is just about 50 percent, so that about half of the letters or words chosen in writing or speaking are under our free choice, and about half are really controlled by the statistical structure of the language.

If someone shouts "GET OUT OF THE ROOM RIGHT ___!" and you don't quite hear the final word, you will most likely be able to infer the missing "NOW" and exit the room. The sentence could lose even more words and still convey its intended signal. You can s ill read th s sente ce even thou h som of th let ers are m ssing.

In games, redundancy is just as important a concept as noise. Consider crossword puzzles. Instead of a Twenty Questions-style single-answer guess, in a crossword puzzle there are many clues, each with its own answer. Yet the answers overlap with each other, and there is redundancy in the system that can "fill in the holes" and compensate for a clue that is too difficult to decipher. The name of an obscure capital city is much easier to figure out when previous answers have already determined some of the letters.

A language must have at least 50 per cent of real freedom (or negative entropy) in the choice of letters if one is to be able to construct satisfactory crossword puzzles. If it has complete freedom, then every array of letters is a crossword puzzle. If it has only 20 per cent of freedom, then it would be impossible to construct crossword puzzles in such complexity and number as would make the game popular. — Warren Weaver, The Mathematical Theory of Communication

Crossword puzzles contain a delicate balance that keeps them just flexible enough to allow for numerous combinations, but still rigid enough so that one correctly answered clue leads to others, and yet others, until the crossword puzzle is solved.

Balancing Act

Information, signals, noise, redundancy: the relationships between games and information theory offer subtle but powerful ways of thinking about design. Successful signal processing requires balancing noise and redundancy. It requires tuning a communication system so that there is enough uncertainty to ensure a sufficient amount of information passing through the channel, but not too much information, so that the signal gets lost in vast oceans of uncertainty.

This is closely linked to systems theory. A complex, emergent system can only exist somewhere between the rigidity of fixed, periodic systems and the hyperflexibility of chaotic systems. Too much structure and a game is overdetermined: there is not enough uncertainty or freedom for the players. Too little structure and the game turns chaotic: there is too much uncertainty, too much freedom, and no sense of how the player's decisions should proceed from one moment to the next.

All of which leads back to meaningful play. Weaver links uncertainty and information to what he calls "freedom" — the ability of a person to make choices in a system. In a system with too much rigidity, a player doesn't have enough freedom: there are choices, but not enough of them, and not enough uncertainty in the system to ever give a player doubt about the outcome. By contrast, in a system where chaos reigns, the system's outcome is perpetually uncertain, and nothing the player does will affect it. In both cases, meaningful play is impossible.

Meaningful play requires that players choose actions from among a palette rich enough to support a large space of possibility but limited enough to properly structure their decisions. Finding the sweet spot between too much and too little freedom — designing constraints that provide enough (but not too much) challenge for players — is an elemental problem of game design. Information theory is one useful way to make sense of these eternal design dilemmas.

Chapter Summary

  • Information theory is a mathematical, structural way of looking at signals that disregards the knowledge-content or meaning of a message. It concerns formal structure, not content.
  • In information theory, information measures uncertainty. A yes/no question contains less information (and less uncertainty) than a question about a favorite color, because there are fewer possible answers to the yes/no question.
  • Information theory studies signal transmission from a source to a target. Part of this process is noise, in which distortion enters the signal from an outside source. Noise increases the uncertainty and therefore the amount of information in a message.
  • Although noise in classical engineering is undesirable, in games noise can be a productive design element. The lusory attitude means players seek out inefficient activities. Noise makes Telephone fun and Charades possible.
  • Redundancy acts to balance out noise by ensuring that not every component of a message is necessary. English contains about 50 percent redundancy. Crossword puzzles exploit redundancy as a core design feature.
  • A game design must strike a balance: too little uncertainty and the system is overdetermined, choices feel hollow; too much uncertainty and the system turns chaotic, choices feel meaningless. Greater freedom of choice, greater uncertainty, and greater information all increase together — and meaningful play depends on finding the right balance.
