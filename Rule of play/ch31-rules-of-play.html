Unit 4: Culture  ·  Chapter 31

Games as Open Culture

Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Salen & Zimmerman

"I've been very influenced by the idea of biological paradigms in design — the notion that instead of designing things, you design processes that generate things." — Brian Eno

Introducing Open Culture

John Conway's Game of Life is not a game in any conventional sense. It has no players, no goals, no winners. It is a cellular automaton — a set of simple rules that generates endlessly complex patterns from an initial configuration. Yet it exemplifies something crucial about open culture: it is a formal system whose outputs cannot be predicted from its inputs, a generative engine whose meaning is produced not by a single author but by the ongoing emergence of its own processes.

The schema Games as Open Culture applies this insight to games and their relationship to players and society. An open culture game is one that extends an invitation beyond the magic circle, encouraging players to participate in the production of the game itself — its content, its meanings, its future forms. The game becomes less a finished artifact than a platform for ongoing cultural creation.

This chapter examines how games can operate as open systems in a cultural sense, exchanging meanings with their surrounding contexts. Two key concepts organize the analysis: the player-as-producer paradigm, in which players become active creators within and around the game, and open source software culture, which provides a model for how games can be designed and distributed as open, community-modifiable systems.

Inventing Jenny: Emergence in Ultima Online

Ultima Online was designed as a fantasy role-playing game — swords and sorcery, quests and guilds. Its designers did not anticipate that players would invent prostitution. Yet within months of the game's launch, an elaborate informal economy of sexual services had emerged, complete with workers, clients, and even a character named Jenny and her companion Pimp Daddy.

This was not a design decision. It was an emergent cultural phenomenon — a form of play that arose from the intersection of the game's formal structures (a persistent world, an economy, player-to-player interaction) with the cultural knowledge and social practices players brought from the world outside. The game's systems were open enough to support meanings their designers never imagined.

The example is significant not because of its specific content but because of what it demonstrates: when games operate as open systems, the cultural meanings they produce are not controlled by their designers. The players — and the culture they carry with them — are co-authors of the game's meaning.

The Player-as-Producer Paradigm

The most influential example of deliberately designed player-as-producer culture is Will Wright's The Sims (2000). Wright conceived the game not as a narrative to be played but as a design tool to be used. His famous "design pyramid" articulated the structure of this vision:

Layer 1: Tools The base of the pyramid — the game engine and editing tools that Maxis provides to players.
Layer 2: Create Players use the tools to create content: custom skins, objects, architectural elements, characters.
Layer 3: Distribute Players share their creations with others through fan sites, community repositories, and peer networks.
Layer 4: Play Other players download and play with the community-produced content, further modifying and remixing it.

According to game journalist J.C. Herz, 90% of all content in The Sims was produced by players rather than by Maxis. Wright had designed not a game but a cultural production system — a platform that turned players into designers. The magic circle became permeable in a new direction: players not only brought culture in, they generated new culture that flowed back out.

Meaningful Production: The Sims Family Album

An unexpected consequence of The Sims' player-as-producer culture was the emergence of a genre that Maxis formalized as the "Family Album" — narrative documentaries created by players using the game as a storytelling medium. Players would stage elaborate scenes, take screenshots, and weave them into stories posted online. The game, designed as a life simulation, became a documentary filmmaking tool, a personal essay medium, and a vehicle for autobiographical reflection.

Players used their Sims families to explore difficult personal experiences — illness, divorce, immigration, sexuality — at one remove, using the game's artificial world as a safe space for narrative processing. The formal structure of a life-simulation game proved extraordinarily hospitable to the raw material of real lives. Culture flowed into the game; the game transformed it into new cultural artifacts that flowed back out.

Open Source Games

The open source software movement, initiated by Richard Stallman and popularized by Linus Torvalds' Linux kernel, established a development model in which code is made freely available, communally modified, and openly shared. Games built on this model share four essential qualities:

Non-hierarchicalContributions come from anyone in the community, not from a single central authority. No designer holds exclusive creative control.
Openly AccessibleThe game's systems — its rules, code, or physical components — are available for anyone to examine, modify, and redistribute.
Non-guidedThe direction of the game's evolution is not predetermined by a single vision but emerges from the cumulative decisions of the community.
EmergentThe game's most significant developments arise not from planned features but from unexpected interactions between community members and the game system.

Game Systems: The Power of a Deck of Cards

The standard deck of playing cards is perhaps the oldest and most successful open source game system. It is a set of components — fifty-two cards in four suits — that has supported hundreds of distinct games across centuries and cultures. No single designer owns the deck. Its evolution has been entirely community-driven. New games emerge from the same components: Poker, Bridge, Solitaire, War, Go Fish, Blackjack, Rummy — each a distinct cultural artifact generated by the same underlying system.

The Icehouse Set, designed by Andrew Looney and John Cooper, deliberately applied this principle to a new physical medium. The set — pyramids of four sizes in five colors — was released with rules for multiple games: Volcano, Zarcana, Zendo, and many others. Rather than selling a single game, Looney sold a game system capable of generating an open-ended ecology of play.

Elemental: Total Conversion and the Limits of Modification

Dungeon Siege, a 2002 fantasy role-playing game by Gas Powered Games, released a robust suite of editing tools allowing players to modify virtually every aspect of the game. Team Elemental — a distributed group of amateur programmers, artists, and level designers from around the world — used these tools not to tweak Dungeon Siege but to transform it into a completely different game. Their mod, Elemental, replaced the Tolkienesque fantasy setting with a "biblepunk" narrative set in an ancient city of Jericho, where former slaves seek retribution.

Gas Powered Games' president Chris Taylor articulated the company's open culture philosophy directly:

"Most of this drive comes from our experience working on Total Annihilation. We learned that a great community could propel a game to new levels of fun and turn it into more than anyone thought it could be. Imagine people all over the world working together to create fantastic new adventures, stories, characters. The possibilities are endless." — Chris Taylor, Gas Powered Games

Telefragging Monster Movies: Machinima

The player-as-producer paradigm sometimes takes modification so far that the resulting activity no longer resembles play at all. Machinima — animated films created using game engine technology — is the most dramatic example of this transformation.

DOOM and Quake were among the first games to offer players open source editors for maps, skins, and gameplay elements. Quake's high-resolution 3D engine attracted a community of modders who quickly developed an economy of community production — maps, skins, character models, weapons — distributed freely online. The competitive practice of speedrunning — racing through levels as quickly as possible and recording the results — generated an archive of game "demos" that players began to use for storytelling purposes.

In August 1996, a Quake clan known as The Rangers made the conceptual leap. They used Quake's built-in demo recording function not to document game play but to create a short film. Their characters were virtual actors; typed text messages were dialogue; a deathmatch map was a film set. "Diary of a Camper" — short, simple, slightly ridiculous — established the genre of machinima.

What followed was a proliferation of machinima production: Scourge Done Slick (1998), Father Frags Best (1999), Devil's Covenant (1998), Anachronox (2001). Web sites — machinima.com, the Quake Movie Library, Planetquake's Cineplex — supported a complete cultural ecology of production, distribution, and criticism.

"Part theater, part film, part computer game, machinima represents the kind of wild exchanges that result from thoroughly transformative play. The cultural play of machinima crosses formal, experiential, and contextual levels, as players playfully repurpose the original structures of the game." — Salen & Zimmerman

Machinima producers challenged and modified the rules of the game at the deepest level — the level of code. By bending and modifying the game's formal structure, they remolded its play into something that only barely resembled its former shape. This creative practice is a form of rule-breaking at the most fundamental level — not cheating within the magic circle but redesigning the circle itself.

Circle Back: Two Directions of Cultural Flow

The model of games as open culture involves cultural production moving in two directions relative to the magic circle:

Outside → In Cultural artifacts produced outside the game — player-made skins, mods, machinima films — flow into the game, being absorbed into the magic circle where they acquire game-specific meaning through play.
Inside → Out Player-created artifacts generated within the game's formal structures — Family Album stories, emergent game strategies, community practices — flow out into the broader culture, acquiring new meanings beyond the magic circle.

Both movements are instances of culturally transformative play. In the first, external culture transforms the game; in the second, the game transforms culture. Designing for open culture means attending to both directions — creating games whose systems are rich enough to invite production and porous enough to allow that production to flow back out into the world.

Key Concepts

  • Games as Open Culture is based on two premises: games are open systems in which play occurs at the cultural level, and games exchange meaning with their surrounding contexts.
  • The player-as-producer paradigm is a design approach in which players are given the tools and opportunity to act as creative producers within the game system.
  • When players become producers, their activities constitute a form of metagaming — interacting with the game outside the bounds of normal play.
  • Open source games are non-hierarchical, openly accessible, non-guided, and emergent — properties that support community-driven cultural production.
  • Game systems (like a deck of cards or the Icehouse Set) are sets of components that support multiple distinct games — an open infrastructure for play.
  • Machinima is the use of game engine technology to create animated films — a form of player production that transforms games into filmmaking platforms.
  • Player-produced modifications flow in two directions: from outside the magic circle in, and from inside the magic circle out.
