Games as the Play of Meaning

How games generate meaning through signs, systems, frames, and metacommunication

Two Kinds of Representation

Games relate to meaning in two distinct ways. First, games can represent — within a game world, objects, characters, and events function as signs that stand for other things. A red health bar represents vitality; a dragon represents a mortal threat. Second, games are representations — the game as a whole is a sign, a depiction of some aspect of reality or imagination. Both forms of representation are operative simultaneously during play, layering meaning upon meaning.

Understanding how meaning works in games requires tools from semiotics — the study of signs. At its simplest, a sign is something that stands for something else. Game designers are, among other things, designers of sign systems: they create worlds of objects, events, and interactions in which everything potentially carries meaning beyond its immediate function.

Systems of Meaning

Game signs do not operate in isolation. They generate meaning through their relationships within a larger system. Consider the health bar in Virtua Fighter 4: a simple horizontal strip of color that tracks a fighter's remaining stamina. Yet this single element is a remarkably dense sign, simultaneously communicating multiple layers of information:

Quantitative State Shows the remaining health as a precise percentage — how close the fighter is to defeat.
Character Identity Indicates which fighter's health is displayed through position and labeling.
Comparative Relationship Communicates the dynamic between the two fighters — who has the advantage.
Game State Provides global information about the match's progress and likely outcome.
Aesthetic Register Communicates the stylized, kinetic world of competitive fighting.
Action Invitation Prompts the player's next decision — attack, defend, or strategize.

A single HUD element carries six distinct layers of meaning simultaneously. This density is not unusual — every element of a well-designed game is similarly overdetermined. The art of game design is in part the art of loading objects, events, and interactions with layers of meaning that inform, engage, and guide the player.

System and Context

Signs do not carry meaning in themselves; they acquire meaning through their position within a system and the context in which they appear. The same signifier can carry radically different meanings depending on its framing.

In Chess, the king piece is an ordinary wooden object. Within the formal structure of Chess, it represents the sovereign. But in the specific context of checkmate — surrounded by enemy pieces with no escape — that same piece acquires a far more charged meaning: defeat, the end of a world, the collapse of an entire system of protection.

The letter "X" written on a page normally means multiplication, an unknown quantity, or a mark of error. Written at the end of a love letter, it means a kiss. Written in Tic-Tac-Toe, it denotes one player's territorial claim. The same mark: three entirely different meanings, each determined by the system and context that surrounds it.

Meaning in games is never fixed — it is always a product of the dynamic relationship between sign, system, and the player who encounters it.

Emergent Representations

One of the most remarkable features of game meaning is its emergent quality. Just as the grammar of a language provides rules for combining a finite set of words into an infinite number of sentences, a game's rule system provides structures for combining a finite set of elements into an infinite range of meaningful experiences.

No two plays of a game are identical in their meaning. The specific configuration of pieces on a Chess board at any moment is unrepeatable; the specific sequence of events in a session of Dungeons & Dragons has never occurred before and will never occur again. These are not just different arrangements of the same elements — they are different meanings, different stories, different experiences.

Emergent representation differs from embedded representation in precisely this way. Embedded representations are pre-authored — a cutscene, a piece of descriptive text, a scripted NPC dialogue. Emergent representations arise from the interaction of system and player, from play itself. The most memorable moments of games — the last-second victory, the catastrophic blunder, the unexpected alliance — are emergent representations that no designer explicitly authored.

The Context of Meaning

For emergent meaning to work, the elements within a game must be clearly legible to players. Designer Neal Hallford describes a common design challenge: how does a player know what a button does before pressing it? Context must do the work that direct description cannot.

In a well-designed game world, the environment itself communicates meaning. A button that opens a door might be placed directly adjacent to the door, so proximity communicates function. Color coding can indicate safety or danger: blue lights signal safe zones, red lights warn of hazards. The consistency of this system — maintained across the entire game — allows players to read the environment the way they read language: not by decoding each sign independently, but by understanding the grammar of the game world as a whole.

This is why integration and discernability are design virtues. When all the elements of a game world are part of a coherent sign system — when every object's appearance communicates something true about its function — the game achieves a kind of semantic elegance that players experience as immersiveness and clarity.

Down the Rabbit Hole

Lewis Carroll's Alice in Wonderland is one of the most sustained literary explorations of what it means to enter a world governed by a different system of meaning. When Alice tumbles down the rabbit hole, she enters a world where the rules of ordinary reality no longer hold — a world that operates according to its own peculiar logic.

Carroll's game sequences are particularly instructive. In the Dodo's race, the participants simply run in circles, stopping when they choose, with everyone declared a winner. The formal structure of a race — competition, winners, losers — has been emptied and replaced with a system where movement and declaration are disconnected from any meaningful outcome.

In the Croquet match in the Queen's Court, flamingos serve as mallets, hedgehogs as balls, playing card soldiers as hoops. The substitution is not arbitrary: it imports the unpredictable, animate behavior of living creatures into a game that normally depends on inert equipment. The result is a game that cannot be played reliably — which is exactly the Queen's point. In Wonderland, the game's formal structure is preserved while its meaningfulness is systematically destroyed.

The magic circle of a game is like Alice's rabbit hole. Crossing its threshold means entering a world with its own rules, its own logic, its own system of meaning — a world in which the ordinary referents of the world outside no longer apply.

Framing Play

Anthropologist and systems theorist Gregory Bateson introduced the concept of the cognitive frame — a psychological structure that defines how we interpret events and communications. A frame is not an explicit set of instructions but an implicit set of assumptions about what kind of thing is happening and what kind of responses are appropriate.

Play is a distinctive cognitive frame. When we enter play, we signal to ourselves and others that what follows is to be interpreted differently from ordinary activity. The frame of play transforms the meaning of actions performed within it: a punch thrown in play is not the same as a punch thrown in anger, even if the physical motion is identical. The frame does the interpretive work.

Game design is, in part, the design of cognitive frames — the creation of contexts that signal to players: you are now in a different kind of space, where different rules and different meanings apply. The title screen, the game board, the magic circle: all are mechanisms for establishing and maintaining the play frame.

Metacommunication and the Double Message

Bateson's most influential contribution to play theory is the concept of metacommunication — communication about communication, messages that indicate how other messages should be interpreted.

Bateson observed that when two young monkeys in a zoo engaged in play fighting, they were doing something paradoxical. One monkey would nip the other. The nip was real — it made physical contact, it was a bite. But it also was not a bite in the ordinary sense, because both monkeys understood that they were playing. The nip communicated two things simultaneously:

The Literal Message "I am biting you." — A real physical action with real physical contact between two animals.
The Meta-Message "This bite means play-bite, not real-bite." — A signal that reframes the action within the play context.

This doubling of meaning is the structural feature of play metacommunication. Play is not the suspension of reality but the simultaneous operation of two levels of reality — the literal and the framed.

Spin the Bottle

The adolescent game Spin the Bottle illustrates this double message with particular clarity. When the bottle stops spinning and points at a player, that player and the spinner must kiss. The kiss is real — it is a physical act between two people. But it is also, within the frame of the game, not a real kiss in the social sense: it was mandated by chance, not by feeling; it occurred within the magic circle of the game, not in ordinary social life. The players can acknowledge both levels at once: "We kissed" and "We didn't really kiss" are simultaneously true.

Cops and Robbers

In Cops and Robbers, when a child playing "robber" is tagged and declared captured, they are captured — the game rule has been invoked, the outcome determined. They must stop running and comply with the game's fictional consequences. But they are also not captured — they are a child standing in a backyard, free to leave at any moment. The capture is real within the game's system of meaning and unreal within the larger context of ordinary life. Both truths coexist, sustained by the cognitive frame of play.

This metacommunicative structure is not a special feature of children's games — it is the universal structure of game play. Every action performed within a game is simultaneously a real action (it changes the state of the game system) and a framed action (it takes place within a context that marks it as different from ordinary action). Understanding this double nature is essential to understanding how games create meaning.

Chapter Summary

  • Games relate to meaning in two ways: games can represent (internal signs) and games are representations (the game as sign).
  • Signs in games acquire meaning through their position within a system and the context in which they appear — not intrinsically.
  • The VF4 health bar illustrates how a single game element can simultaneously carry multiple layers of meaning.
  • Game meaning is partly emergent — arising from the interaction of system and player, not pre-authored by the designer.
  • The magic circle of a game is a threshold: crossing it means entering a world governed by its own system of meaning.
  • Gregory Bateson's cognitive frame describes the play frame as a context that transforms the meaning of all actions performed within it.
  • Metacommunication is the double message of play: every action is simultaneously real (it affects the game state) and framed (it takes place within the play context). Bateson's monkeys, Spin the Bottle, and Cops and Robbers all exemplify this structure.
