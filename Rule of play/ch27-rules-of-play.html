Games as the Play of Simulation

Procedural representation, the immersive fallacy, and the double-consciousness of play

Introducing Simulation

Every game is a simulation. From Chess to Centipede to Dungeons & Dragons, every game represents some aspect of reality — physical, social, economic, or imaginary — through the formal structure of its rules. The schema Games as the Play of Simulation examines this representational dimension of games in depth, asking: what does it mean for a game to simulate something, and how does that simulation create meaningful play?

Working Definition A simulation is a procedural representation of aspects of "reality."

This definition has two key components. Procedural: the representation works through processes, rules, and dynamic systems rather than static images or text. Reality: the simulation has a referent — something it depicts, even if that something is fictional, fantastical, or highly abstracted. The quotation marks around "reality" acknowledge that simulations can represent both the actual world and imaginary worlds. A simulation of medieval combat and a simulation of dragon-slaying are both simulations in this sense.

Game vs Non-Game Simulations

Many simulations are not games: flight training simulators, architectural walkthroughs, weather prediction models. These share the procedural, representational character of game simulations but lack the particular structures — goals, rules, uncertainty, outcomes — that make something a game.

At the same time, all games can be understood as simulations, even those that seem maximally abstract. Tetris simulates a world of falling geometric forms and spatial constraint. Chess simulates military conflict with a highly stylized abstraction. Tic-Tac-Toe simulates territorial competition. What these examples share is a procedural representation of some underlying dynamic, however distant that dynamic is from everyday experience.

Game simulations usually operate metaphorically: they do not literally recreate a representation of their subject matter. The difference between a game simulation and its referent can be a source of pleasure for players.

Procedural Representation

The key concept is procedural representation: representation that emerges from the procedures of game play rather than from static images or descriptions. Procedural representation is how simulations simulate their subject matter — not by depicting it as a picture would, but by enacting a dynamic model of it.

Three examples show how games represent their subjects through procedure rather than appearance:

Diplomacy

The board game Diplomacy simulates the political maneuvering of European great powers through the mechanics of private negotiation, simultaneous revelation, and contingent outcomes. Players make secret deals and commitments to each other, then reveal their orders at the same time — often to discover that the ally they trusted has betrayed them. Switzerland is the only country not on the map as a playable territory: a procedural representation of neutrality through exclusion from the game system.

The simulation of political negotiation does not emerge from any image of a map room or diplomat — it emerges from the procedure itself: the private talks, the public reveals, the collapsed alliances.

Vampire New Games

In this New Games encounter, one player is a vampire who must move through a crowd of other players with eyes closed, trying to touch them. Caught players stumble away, arms outstretched. Other vampires are created as they are caught. The simulation of the undead spreading through a population is entirely procedural — it emerges from a set of behavioral rules, not from costumes or descriptions.

Illuminati

In Steve Jackson's card game Illuminati, certain rules permit players to secretly violate the formal rules of the game. This rule-breaking is built into the system — it is permitted, paradoxically, by the rules themselves. The procedural representation of conspiracy and power subversion emerges not from narrative description but from this structural anomaly within the game's rule system.

Represented Conflict

Games typically simulate some form of conflict. The subject matter of game representations is linked to the kinds of conflict that a game can represent. Three broad categories capture most of game subject matter:

Territorial Conflict Struggle over space. Chess, Go, Tic-Tac-Toe, Football, Soccer. The game simulates the claiming, defending, and contesting of territory.
Economic Conflict Competition over limited resources or economic advantage. Pinball (score as resource), Magic: The Gathering (cards and mana), Poker (chips and hands).
Conflict over Knowledge Competition structured around information, learning, or communication. Trivial Pursuit, Hollywood Squares, Charades.

Most games combine two or all three of these categories. A real-time strategy game simulates territorial conflict (map control), economic conflict (resource gathering), and potentially knowledge conflict (fog of war, scouting). It is also possible to represent other forms of conflict — social, cultural, emotional — though these are less common in commercial game design.

Procedural Characters

Characters in games are procedural representations as well. A game character is not primarily a visual or literary construct — it is a set of behaviors, parameters, and interactions that define how the character functions in the game system. Four examples illustrate the range:

Link's Awakening — The Witch Defined by her isolated location, difficult to reach; her single core procedure: bring her a mushroom and she transforms it into magic powder. The meaning of this character comes from the total game context, not from her description.
Virtua Fighter 4 — Pai Chan An action film star whose procedural identity is speed and emotional intensity — these attributes produce a fighting style of quick, chained combos. Her "character" is not backstory but behavior.
Deus Ex — Agent Types AI characters defined by behavioral parameter tables: Civilian (low threat response, high flee threshold), Thug (moderate aggression), Military (high aggression, coordinated response). Emergent character from numerical parameters.
Blob — New Games One player begins as "It." When tagged, players join hands, growing a collective blob of players that must catch others together. The character of "Blob" is entirely procedural — it is constituted by the growing chain.

Designing Simulations

Game designer Warren Robinett identifies four essential properties that every game simulation must embody. Because designing a simulation means radically reducing its subject matter, a designer must carefully select which aspects to depict:

Abstract Every simulation leaves out more than it includes. Abstraction is not a limitation but a design choice: what matters, and what does not?
Numerical Simulations translate qualitative phenomena into numerical parameters. The richness of a character becomes a set of attributes; the complexity of terrain becomes a movement cost.
Limited A simulation cannot be both broad and deep. Every choice to model one dimension in detail means modeling another dimension more shallowly.
Systemic The elements of a simulation interact as a system. The whole is greater than — and often different from — the sum of its parts.

Case-Based vs Generalized Simulation

A critical design choice in structuring a simulation is whether to use a case-based approach (specifying each relationship individually) or a generalized approach (creating systemic rules that govern classes of relationships). Three game designers — Hallford and Hallford, Richard Rouse III, and Harvey Smith — each advocate for generalized simulation design and identify three advantages:

Decreases Work Time A generalized system — objects that cause damage, doors that respond to damage — allows categories of relationships rather than specifying every pair. Adjustments ripple through the class, not through every instance.
Increases Emergence More flexible simulations produce more emergent behavior. Birds that react to sound, not just proximity, create the possibility of a flock scattering at a gunshot — an emergent event no designer explicitly authored.
Increases Play Options When a dungeon pressure plate responds to any object of sufficient weight, players who lack the obvious solution can find others: a magical snowstorm that creates enough weight. Idiosyncratic play styles become possible.

Yet neither approach is universally superior. Marc LeBlanc's work on Thief shows why balance is necessary. Water extinguishing fire 100% of the time is emulation — a case-based rule with no simulation. But the way light from a torch affects the player's visibility is a full generalized simulation of light physics. The two approaches coexist within the same game: LeBlanc's point is that simulations can and should be embedded within each other at different levels of generality.

The Value of Reality: The Car Wars Anecdote

At the 1998 Game Developers Conference, Steve Jackson described a development team that created an extraordinarily detailed driving simulation for a combat racing game based on his paper game Car Wars. The simulation incorporated minute details of real driving physics and a track based precisely on an existing speedway. Yet the developers couldn't reach the top speeds of the car they were simulating. When a professional race car driver visited and sat down at the prototype, he immediately drove around the track at close to real-world speed record pace.

The lesson is not that the simulation was wrong — it was that the designers had forgotten the player. A game simulation not only includes the formal mechanisms of the system, but also the ways those mechanisms engender player action. The rules are always set in motion within an experiential context that includes particular players with their own levels of desire, skill, and expectation. The Car Wars designers had created a certain space of possibility, but it took the right kind of player to navigate that space in the way it was meant to be explored.

Framing the Simulation

To understand the full complexity of simulation in games, we must return to Gregory Bateson's concept of metacommunication. When two dogs engage in play fighting, one nips the other. The nip signals a bite — and simultaneously signals that it is not a real bite. This double message is the structure of all play.

In simulation, this double-consciousness is always operative. A kiss in Spin the Bottle and a frag in a Quake deathmatch both refer to real-world actions — kissing and killing — and simultaneously communicate: I'm not kissing or killing you. I'm just playing. Bateson illustrates this paradoxical state of mind with a schematic:

All statements within this frame are untrue.

I love you.

I hate you.

This is a riff on Epimenides' Paradox — the Liar's Paradox. The first statement, All statements within this frame are untrue, locates the paradox within a frame: a limited context within which the paradoxical sentence asserts its meaning. The magic circle of a game is exactly such a frame. It is both a prerequisite and an effect of play — robust, yet fragile; sustained by the very metacommunicative assertions it makes possible.

The Immersive Fallacy

All forms of entertainment strive to create suspension of disbelief, a state in which the player's mind forgets that it is being subjected to entertainment and instead accepts what it perceives as reality. — François Dominic Laramée, "Immersion"

Game designer Frank Lantz calls ideas like Laramée's "widely held but seldom examined." Salen and Zimmerman call them the immersive fallacy: the idea that the pleasure of a media experience lies in its ability to sensually transport the participant into an illusory, simulated reality — one so complete that the frame falls away and the player truly believes they are part of an imaginary world.

The immersive fallacy drives much thinking about the future of gaming: the belief that as simulation technology advances, games will approach the Star Trek Holodeck — total sensory immersion indistinguishable from reality. But this view misunderstands how play actually works.

A player is always aware, on some level, that they are playing. A teen kissing in Spin the Bottle, a Gran Turismo player driving a virtual race car — both understand that their play references other realities, and both simultaneously know they are participating within a constructed reality. The very thing that makes their activity play is this double awareness. Players are not immersed in a simulated reality; they are immersed in meaning.

Film scholar Elena Gorfinkel makes the point precisely: immersion is not a property intrinsic to a media object but an effect it produces. One can get immersed in Tetris, a game with no sensory replication of reality whatsoever. Immersion into game play is at least as important as immersion into a game's representational space.

Remediating Games

Media theorists Jay David Bolter and Richard Grusin offer a more nuanced model in their concept of remediation. All media function through a double logic: on one hand, immediacy — the promise to authentically reproduce the world and create an alternative reality; on the other hand, hypermediacy — the unavoidable reminder that the experience is constructed and artificial.

New media don't simply replace old media with more "immersive" experiences. They oscillate between immediacy and hypermediacy — each promise of transparency leads to awareness of the medium itself. A webcam promises immediacy through live access, but the interface, browser, and operating system remind users of the construction.

Games operate through this same double logic. In Cops and Robbers, players willingly take on theatrical roles — but they also know they are players in a game. In Halo, the sensory vertigo of navigating a coherent 3D space coexists with an awareness of the interface, server speeds, and cheat codes. The immersive fallacy grossly overemphasizes immediacy while ignoring the hypermediacy that is equally part of the experience.

The Character of Character

The complexity of simulation is particularly visible in the relationship between player and character. The immersive fallacy would suggest that playing a character means becoming that character — that in an ideal game, the player would lose themselves completely in the character. But this misrepresents the actual phenomenology of play.

Psychologist Gary Alan Fine, in his study of tabletop role-playing communities Shared Fantasies, identifies three simultaneous layers of consciousness that players inhabit:

Person
The player grounded in the primary framework — the commonsense understanding of the real world. This frame does not depend on other frameworks but on the ultimate reality of events. The player is always present as a person connected to the real world.
Player
The player whose actions are governed by a complicated set of rules and constraints. They manipulate their characters with knowledge of the game's structure, sharing approximately the same knowledge as other players.
Character
The player who not only manipulates a character but is that character. The character identity, separate from the player identity, inhabits the game's simulated world.

Two examples show how these three layers coexist in real game situations:

Tomb Raider

Our hypothetical player is immersed in the game's narrative world, taking on the identity of Lara Croft with her requisite strengths and weaknesses (I feel lost... I can't believe I survived that trap!). Simultaneously, he views her from behind, pushing buttons and manipulating her like a puppet on his quest for the next level (What was that cheat code again? This cutscene sucks.). He is both character and player. The larger social context — impressing a friend, preparing notes for a lecture — constitutes the player as person.

BattleBots

The BattleBots player's character is a battling, remote-controlled robot. The glass between player and game is not a television screen but a sheet of plexi protecting players from flying scraps of metal. During play, the player might switch between the character/player/person frames many times — moving between emotional identification with his robot (Ouch! I just got slammed!) and his role as player (Let's see if I can get my bot out of the corner). He might even break the frame of player to wave to a friend in the crowd or offer a sound bite to the television host.

Fine's three-layer model is an extension of the double-consciousness of play. Players always know they are playing, and in that knowledge are free to move among the roles of person, player, and character. This movement is not a failure of immersion to be overcome — it is responsible for some of the unique pleasures that emerge from a game.

Hacking the Holodeck

The immersive fallacy is not merely a mistaken idea — it is a stumbling block to advances in game design. As long as designers pine for the technology of the holodeck, they lack the vision to appreciate the potential for game innovation in the double-consciousness of play today.

What if game designers focused their efforts on actively playing with the double-consciousness of play rather than attempting to eliminate it? Games that encourage players to shift the frame, questioning what is inside or outside the game. Games that play with the lamination between player and character. Games that emphasize metagaming, or that connect the magic circle so closely with external contexts that the game appears synchronous with everyday life.

Robust contemporary pop culture forms — hip-hop, fashion, anime — are not premised on naive ideas of immersion. They have a deep understanding of the way media cultivates immersion while making explicit the mechanisms through which the representation is experienced. Games have only just scratched the surface of what they can represent. The procedural representation of new kinds of game content is within our grasp, but new content can only be discovered by paying attention to the fundamental principles of game design and meaningful play.

Chapter Summary

  • A simulation is a procedural representation of aspects of "reality." All games are simulations; simulations represent their subjects through process and dynamic rules rather than static images.
  • Game simulations typically represent territorial conflict, economic conflict, or conflict over knowledge — most games combine two or all three categories.
  • Procedural representation is how simulations simulate: Diplomacy represents political negotiation through private talks and simultaneous reveals; Vampire New Games represents undead spread through behavioral contagion rules.
  • Simulations are abstract, numerical, limited, and systemic. Designing a simulation means carefully selecting which aspects of a phenomenon to depict.
  • Simulations can be structured through case-based logic (every relationship specified individually) or generalized logic (shared attributes govern classes of objects). Generalized structures decrease work time, increase emergence, and increase play options — but a balance of both is usually necessary.
  • Metacommunication means that players are always aware of the frame of a game. A player's state of mind embodies a double-consciousness: it both accepts and refutes the simulated reality simultaneously.
  • The immersive fallacy is the mistaken belief that the pleasure of a media experience lies in transporting a player into an illusory reality. It ignores the metacommunicative nature of play.
  • Bolter and Grusin's remediation describes how all media combine immediacy (transparent reproduction of reality) and hypermediacy (awareness of the constructed medium). Games operate through both simultaneously.
  • Gary Alan Fine identifies three layers of player consciousness: direct identification with the game character, engagement with game procedures as a player, and existence in larger social contexts as a person. Movement among these frames is a feature, not a failure, of game play.
