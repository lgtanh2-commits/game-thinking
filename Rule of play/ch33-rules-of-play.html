Games as Cultural Environment

Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Salen & Zimmerman

"A game is a system in which players engage in an artificial conflict, defined by rules, that results in a quantifiable outcome." — the definition that opened this book — now under examination

Raise the Red Flag

With that single sentence, many pages ago, Rules of Play began. Since then, the definition has served as a reliable foundation across hundreds of pages of analysis. But this final schema — Games as Cultural Environment — may force a reevaluation. After three units of exploration, one element of the definition begins to look increasingly suspect.

It is not the idea that games are systems, nor that they have rules and outcomes, nor that through games players engage in a conflict. The element that seems increasingly unstable is the idea that games are artificial — that they are removed from ordinary life.

Games framed as cultural environments are not separate from their surroundings. They participate in the ebb and flow of culture itself, blurring the distinction between inside and outside the magic circle to the point that the circle's boundary can become genuinely difficult to locate. These are games in which the space of play is so thoroughly integrated with the space of life that the artificiality of games begins to dissolve.

Introducing Cultural Environment

The most familiar examples of games that bleed into their cultural environment include Assassin (also known as Killer) — a game in which players on college campuses stalked, hunted, and evaded each other with dart guns over days or weeks of real time, the play taking place not in an isolated game space but in and among the activities of daily life. More recent examples include Majestic, a large-scale experimental game by Electronic Arts in which players received game-related phone calls and voicemails in the middle of the night, and Botfighters, a cell phone game that tracked players' physical locations and challenged them to unexpected duels.

These games raise fundamental questions: How does play change when the difference between "inside" and "outside" the game is ambiguous? How permeable can the boundary between game world and real world become? And if that boundary erases itself entirely, is what remains still a game?

Back to Basics: Implicit Rules and the Lusory Attitude

The analysis of games as cultural environments returns to foundational concepts. Each game is composed of three kinds of rules: constituative rules (the logical, mathematical structures of the formal system), operational rules (the practical rules of play that direct player action), and implicit rules (the unwritten rules that players bring to every game — rules that are usually unstated and must be inferred or intuited).

Philosopher Stephen Sniderman identifies four forms of implicit knowledge that players must bring to even the simplest game:

EtiquetteUnwritten but sometimes stated traditions associated with the game that do not necessarily affect the play itself — appropriateness of talking, gloating, replaying a point, giving advice.
EthosUnwritten and rarely expressed assumptions about how to interpret and enforce the "written" rules — palming in basketball; the strike zone in American and National leagues; the footfault in tennis.
ConventionsUnwritten and generally unstatable customs related to playing, competing, winning/losing — taking the game with the appropriate seriousness, playing "hard" regardless of the score.
ContextThe social, cultural, economic, political, and moral consequences of the result — whether someone's livelihood or self-esteem depends on the outcome.

These implicit rules arise from cultural custom, tradition, and player experience. They directly link the formal and cultural aspects of a game, creating a bridge between the forms of authority that exist inside and outside its space of play. The magic circle is not just a formal structure — it is also a cultural institution.

The lusory attitude — philosopher Bernard Suits' term for the state of mind in which players consciously accept the artificial authority of game rules — extends to implicit rules as well. To play a game is not just to follow its operational rules but to accept the cultural framework those rules inhabit. The magic circle of any game extends beyond its formal boundaries to embrace the cultural conventions that make play possible.

Case Study 1: A.I. — "The Beast"

In 2001, Microsoft launched a web-based game as a viral marketing campaign for Steven Spielberg's film A.I.: Artificial Intelligence. Known informally as "The Beast" or "The A.I. Game," it invited players from around the world to collaboratively decipher cryptic puzzles and clues distributed across dozens of websites. The game began with an enigmatic credit for "Jeanine Salla, Sentient Machine Therapist" at the end of the film's preview trailer — a breadcrumb that, when followed, began a Wonderland-style journey through linked websites blending real-world information with the fictive world of the film.

The game made use of several design strategies that deliberately erased the boundary between game world and real world:

Web-basedThe web itself was the game's primary medium — players searched and surfed as gameplay, making the internet fundamental to the game's structure.
Fiction as RealityNo page in the game announced "this is fiction." Many websites could have been mistaken for real ones — reinforcing the illusion that the game was part of the real world.
DecentralizedNo single gateway or homepage. Content spread across many websites, with player community site cloudmakers.org emerging as the primary hub.
Offline EventsEmail, faxes, phone calls — and three real-life rallies held by the "Anti-Robot Militia" in New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles — brought game events into physical space.
EpisodicContent updated weekly, with a mounting sense of urgency; the game could never be put on pause.
Distributed SolvingMany puzzles were impossible to solve alone. Fan sites became essential infrastructure for collaborative problem-solving.

Players were profoundly affected by the dissolution of the game's boundaries. As one player wrote at cloudmakers.org: "Here we are, every one of us excited at blurring the lines between story and reality. The game promises to become not just entertainment, but our lives." The ambiguity surrounding the game's status — was it a game, a puzzle, a story, an evil marketing ploy? — made the experience oddly compelling. Another player noted the game's reliance on metacommunication itself as a form of play: players were simultaneously believing and skeptical, playing a game and playing real life, and the tension between these frames drove the game forward.

Case Study 2: Seasons of Darkness — The Invisible Playground

Nick Fortugno ran a Live-Action Role-Playing Game (LARP) based on Vampire: The Masquerade in New York City for many years. His game, Seasons of Darkness, met regularly in public urban spaces ranging from Washington Square Park to Grand Central Station. Players took the roles of vampires — ancient creatures living secretly among humans — and engaged in baroque power struggles between aristocratic vampire clans.

The LARP blurred the magic circle through several interrelated design decisions:

Public Spaces

Most LARP sessions were held not in isolated settings but in public urban spaces. Fortugno (and his co-administrator Tami Meyers) created a game that intrinsically blurred the magic circle — appropriating "found" contexts into the gameplay. A balcony overlooking the World Trade Center's Winter Garden might heighten the dramatic effect of a speech; the same balcony might serve as a vantage point for spying. The game's space of possibility — already expansive within a closed magic circle — became truly infinite.

Real-World Interaction

Seasons of Darkness players made use of naturalized social behaviors. Touching another player on the arm had formal game ramifications — but in a LARP, this gesture has the same communicative meaning it does in everyday life: empathy, a request to stop speaking. The core mechanics of the game overlapped with the behaviors of everyday life, making play inseparable from ordinary social interaction.

Emergent Storytelling

Rather than relying on pre-generated storylines, Seasons of Darkness was a largely emergent narrative system. Fortugno encouraged bottom-up rather than top-down narratives: players scheming and plotting against one another produced the most significant story events. Each session was a complex system in which narrative particles collided and combined. Fortugno had to balance emergent narrative with embedded narrative elements — unexpected developments kept the game vital, but elaborately developed embedded plots were only fully realized over years of play.

Meta-Narratives and the Secret of the Magic Circle

In the narrative universe of the game, vampires live in secret, pretending to be human — the "masquerade" of the game's subtitle. Players speaking about vampire clan politics lower their voices when non-players walk nearby, maintaining the in-game narrative of secrecy. At the same time, players maintain another form of secret: the fact that they are playing a game at all. The game secret — that a player with crossed arms is "invisible," that a tap on the shoulder has supernatural meaning — is kept from the general public.

There is a beautiful double logic here. Just as vampires in the fictional world keep their existence secret, players of the game keep the very presence of the magic circle secret. The special information players share about the existence of the game is cultural information — it defines the play community and binds it together more than the formal rules do.

Case Study 3: Suspicion — Ideological Environment

Suspicion is an unpublished card game designed by Eric Zimmerman for an office environment, to be played over a week of real time. In a company of about a hundred employees, approximately 20 players received game rules and a small collection of cards. One card contained each player's identity: membership in two groups (a Sect and an Institution), each unique. Players also received six Stash Cards bearing the color and insignia of the groups in the game.

Each player's goal was to locate other members of their groups, accumulate Stash Cards matching their groups' colors, and formally accuse other players (using Accusation Cards) of group membership — triggering a simple dueling card game. The first Sect and Institution to acquire all their members and a matching set of Stash Cards won.

Lived Conflict

Unlike Seasons of Darkness, Suspicion's "public space" was the players' actual workplace: their offices, lunchrooms, and conference rooms. The game truly colonized its environment. When a player arrived at work, he or she had to be ready to attack or be attacked. All games embody a conflict, and the magic circle normally contains that conflict. In Suspicion, there was no escape from the game conflict — the play of the game had to be integrated into the rest of one's life.

Interventions Shaking It Up

Each player's attempt to reveal his own code word or gesture to find allies — while preventing members of other groups from noticing — added a new layer of meaning to every in-office speech-act. Is the person you're talking to playing the game? Are they trying to tell you something? Have you unintentionally let your code word slip? The sense of altered consciousness was so pervasive that even workers not playing the game joined in, pretending they too had secret identities.

Suspicion was designed to undercut existing power relationships at work. When players were randomly assigned to Sects and Institutions, the makeup of these groups had nothing to do with the existing departmental, spatial, economic, or authoritative relationships among players. Suspicion reshuffled and thereby transformed these power relations. The game also introduced forbidden play — forms of interaction not permitted in ordinary life: a worker might accuse her boss of being an enemy and attack him mercilessly with Stash Cards. By recasting company authority as a tangled web of deception, relationships were radically transformed.

During the climax of one playtest, one player made an offer to pay another cash for her Stash Cards. The mere possibility of real money crossing the magic circle caused intense emotions. The game was in danger of imploding — the designer implemented a rule outlawing the use of real-world money. The anecdote illustrates both the power and the challenge of games as cultural environments: a transformative political statement about the corporate workplace, Suspicion succeeded in making play genuinely pleasurable while engaging in a cultural critique — but cultural environments are always home to someone, and even a radically resistant game must maintain a spirit of fair play toward those it impacts.

The Artificial Question

We return at last to the question of artificiality. Does the magic circle disappear when games are framed as cultural environments? In each of our three case studies — A.I., Seasons of Darkness, and Suspicion — the answer is: not entirely.

In A.I., players never forgot that the game was really a promotion for a Hollywood film. In Seasons of Darkness, game sessions took place within strictly delimited physical and temporal boundaries — every session began and ended with a Peter Pan-inspired ritual in which Fortugno blew imaginary pixie dust over the players. In Suspicion, the restriction on using real money nudged the game toward a more closed system. In each case, although the magic circle blurred, shifted, and blended with its environment, it still in some way remained intact.

Calling games artificial does not mean that they are wholly distinct from culture. No matter how integrated into culture games might be, there will always be some aspect of a game's operation that relies on its own system — rather than that of culture — to create meanings for players. Scrabble makes use of language borrowed from its cultural environment, but only in the highly specialized context of the game itself do we assign Scrabble points to letters. Scrabble will always maintain its artificial status in some measure. That is simply part of what makes it a game.

Final Framings

"Radical innovation and the design of meaningful play can only be accomplished by paying attention to the fundamental design principles that apply to all games. Even when you dissect or design breakthrough games that bend, break, and transform traditional gaming structures, the fundamentals of game design remain." — Salen & Zimmerman

There is a widespread impulse among those who study games to want to leapfrog over the basics — to immediately break all the rules, violate the magic circle, turn players into designers, and radically reinvent the phenomenon of games. The games studied in this final schema demonstrate that blurring the magic circle to question the fundamental properties of games can indeed lead to new kinds of play experiences. But the abstraction and complexity of this phenomenon was best deciphered by a return to game design fundamentals, to the core language of meaningful play, to the concepts that constitute the Rules of Play.

For every A.I., Seasons of Darkness, or Suspicion, there are hundreds of unsuccessful game designs: online games that never found an audience, LARPs that fizzled out before they got off the ground, experiments that were simply not fun to play. Games as Cultural Environments takes us to the edge of the void — it lets us consider a game that undermines its own existence by creating the means for its erasure. Returning from that edge requires the fundamentals: the vocabulary of meaningful play that this book has spent its pages building.

Key Concepts

  • Games as Cultural Environment explores games that blur the space inside and outside the magic circle, creating ambiguity between players and non-players and overlap between game and real life.
  • Implicit rules — etiquette, ethos, convention, and context — are forms of cultural authority that link the artificiality of games to the real-world contexts they inhabit.
  • The lusory attitude extends to implicit rules: accepting a game's authority means accepting its cultural framework, not just its formal rules. The magic circle embraces larger cultural domains.
  • A.I., Seasons of Darkness, and Suspicion are all games that operate as cultural environments — in each case, meaningful play arises because careful game design maintains the magic circle in some way, even as it plays with the possibility of erasing it.
  • Considered as a cultural environment, a game plays with the possible erasure of the magic circle — and therefore plays with the possibility of its own existence. Some semblance of the magic circle always remains.
  • Games are always in some way artificial — they are always capable of producing internal game meanings — even when deeply integrated into culture. Games are artificial because they are designed.
  • Sophisticated game analysis requires drawing on the full vocabulary of RULES, PLAY, and CULTURE simultaneously. No single schema is sufficient on its own.
