Games as Systems of Uncertainty

Chance, probability, and randomness — why uncertainty is not a flaw but the engine of meaningful play.

Introducing Uncertainty

The spirit of play is the spirit of not knowing. Play is the place where certainty dissolves and surprise becomes possible. — Bernie DeKoven, The Well-Played Game

Uncertainty is not an optional component of games — it is a structural necessity. If the outcome of a game is completely predetermined, player actions have no effect on the result. And if player actions have no effect on the result, meaningful play is impossible. This is the direct link between uncertainty and the core concept that frames the book.

Uncertainty operates in games at two distinct levels simultaneously:

Macro-Level Uncertainty The uncertainty of the game as a whole: who will win? Which strategy will prevail? How the overall arc of a particular play session will unfold. This is the large-scale unpredictability of the game system, present from start to finish.
Micro-Level Uncertainty The uncertainty of individual moments: what card will be drawn? Will this die roll succeed? What move will my opponent make? These are the local, turn-by-turn uncertainties that add up to create the macro-level experience.

Both levels are necessary. A game with macro-level certainty (a determined winner) but micro-level uncertainty (individual chance events) still fails to produce meaningful play — the interesting moments lead nowhere. A game with macro-level uncertainty but no micro-level chance events may or may not be interesting, depending on whether skill creates sufficient uncertainty at the large scale. Most successful games maintain uncertainty at both levels simultaneously.

Three Degrees of Uncertainty

Drawing on Richard Epstein's The Theory of Gambling and Statistical Logic, the chapter identifies three distinct categories that describe the relationship between a game decision and its outcome:

Certainty A completely predetermined outcome. The player knows exactly what will happen as a result of their action. Certain outcomes contain no information — no reduction of uncertainty occurs. Games of pure certainty (if such games exist) are not really games at all, since player choices are irrelevant to outcomes.
Risk An outcome with a known probability of happening. The player does not know which outcome will occur, but they know the probability distribution over possible outcomes. Roulette is a canonical example: every bet has a precisely calculable expected value. Risk is quantifiable uncertainty.
Uncertainty An outcome whose probability is unknown to the player. The player cannot calculate expected values because the underlying probability distribution is not known. Online Chess against an anonymous opponent involves uncertainty about opponent strength: there is no known probability that any particular move will succeed.

Most real games combine all three categories. A Chess player faces certainty about piece movement rules, risk about tactical calculations they can evaluate (with known outcomes), and uncertainty about the opponent's future intentions and capabilities. The mixture of all three — and the shifting balance between them as a game unfolds — is characteristic of rich play experiences.

The Feeling of Randomness

One of the most subtle and important concepts in the chapter is the distinction between actual randomness and the feeling of randomness. These are not the same thing, and conflating them leads to serious design errors.

Chinese Checkers: Crowded Center Illusion

In Chinese Checkers, all players' pieces converge toward the center of the board early in the game. Players frequently report that the center "feels chaotic" — that outcomes seem random and their decisions don't matter. But the center is not random: every move is deterministic, and skilled players consistently outperform novices. The feeling of randomness comes from the complexity of tracking many pieces and interactions simultaneously, not from any actual randomness in the game system.

SiSSYFiGHT 2000: Social Uncertainty

The online game SiSSYFiGHT 2000 involves multiple players (four to six) simultaneously choosing actions targeting each other. The game mechanics are deterministic — no dice, no card draws. But with five players choosing targets simultaneously without communication, outcomes feel wildly random. Again, the feeling comes from the complexity of the social dynamics (modeling five other players' intentions simultaneously), not from any mechanism of chance.

The design lesson is significant: the feeling of randomness is a real design resource, independent of actual randomness. Strategic complexity, social complexity, and information asymmetry can all produce a feeling of randomness without introducing any actual probabilistic elements. Conversely, a game with extensive probability mechanics may feel highly deterministic if players can easily model the probabilities.

A game with too little feeling of randomness can feel dry and overly competitive. A game with too much can make players feel powerless. There is no formula for the right amount; the key is to create meaningful play that takes unique advantage of the game's formal structure.

Probability in Games

The word probability comes from the Latin probare — "to test" or "to prove." The mathematical theory of probability emerged from practical questions about gambling in 17th-century France, when Blaise Pascal began corresponding with Pierre de Fermat about how to fairly divide the stakes of an interrupted game. From these letters grew one of the most powerful branches of mathematics.

In games, probability plays two fundamental roles: it introduces uncertainty into game outcomes, and it gives players a framework for reasoning about that uncertainty. Players who understand probability can make better decisions under risk — which is exactly what meaningful play requires.

Dice Probability

The most common probability mechanism in tabletop games is dice. A standard six-sided die has six equally likely outcomes, each with probability 1/6 (approximately 16.67%). This is the baseline for most dice-based probability reasoning in game design.

When two dice are rolled and summed, the probability space becomes more interesting. There are 36 equally likely outcomes (6 × 6), but the sums are not equally distributed:

Sum 23456 7 89101112
Ways to Roll 12345 6 54321
Probability 1/362/363/364/365/36 6/36 5/364/363/362/361/36

Seven is the most probable sum (6 out of 36 possible rolls — 16.67%), while 2 and 12 each occur only once in 36 rolls (2.78%). This distribution is the mathematical backbone of Monopoly's movement system: the board's 40 spaces, combined with an average roll of 7, means a player completes a full circuit roughly every 5.7 turns. The probability distribution is not incidental to the game — it shapes the entire flow of play.

Three Formal Pleasures of Pure-Chance Games

Against the view that games of pure chance (like Chutes and Ladders) have no value, the authors identify three genuine formal pleasures available even in completely deterministic-outcome games:

Dramatic Tension Even without meaningful choices, the sequence of chance events creates narrative tension — will the next spin be good or bad? — that is genuinely experienced as suspense, especially by younger players.
Social Ritual The shared experience of watching chance unfold together — reactions to good and bad luck, the collective buildup to a spin — creates social bonding and shared emotional experience.
Learning the Game Form Simple chance games teach the basic conventions of game-playing: taking turns, moving pieces, reading a board, accepting a determined outcome. These are genuine skills for young players encountering games for the first time.

Case Study: Thunderstorm

Thunderstorm (called Gewitter in Germany) is a family dice game that illustrates how pure chance can be choreographed to produce genuine dramatic engagement.

The rules: four to eight players each take a turn rolling six dice. If you roll at least one 1, you set aside all the 1s and pass the remaining dice to the next player. If you roll no 1s, a line is drawn on your "house" — a six-line drawing. When your house is complete and you fail again, you are out. Last player remaining wins.

Thunderstorm is a game of pure chance — there are no decisions. Yet the formal structure creates a compelling dramatic rhythm through overlapping cycles:

Dice Reduction Cycle As 1s are removed from the pool, each player rolls fewer dice — increasing risk. When a player rolls a 1 with all six dice, the next player gets a fresh full set. This ebb and flow of dice creates a pulse to the game.
House-Building Progression Each player's house fills at their own pace, creating a race-like narrative visible to all. The diagram of each player's house provides a constant comparative record — who is closest to elimination?

All of this complexity arises from a game of pure chance — no betting, no decisions. Thunderstorm demonstrates that a rich chance-based system can generate surprisingly meaningful play through careful design of the uncertainty structure itself.

Case Study: Pig

Where Thunderstorm is pure chance, Pig introduces a single meaningful choice into a chance framework — and the result is a game of striking strategic depth.

Rules: players take turns rolling one die and accumulating points. If you roll a 1, your turn ends and your accumulated points for that turn are lost. If you roll any other number, you add it to your turn total. At any point, you can choose to stop rolling and bank your turn total. First player to reach 100 wins.

The single choice — roll again or stop — sits at the nexus of two competing forces: the desire to accumulate more points and the increasing probability of losing everything accumulated. Reiner Knizia calculates the probability of survival and expected total points over successive rolls:

Knizia suggests stopping at 20 points as a reasonable baseline strategy. But Pig's full strategic depth emerges when players consider each other's positions. If you are behind, you may need to press further to catch up — accepting more risk for more potential gain. If you are ahead, you can afford to play more conservatively. The moment-to-moment decision exists within the larger context of everyone's score.

Pig is an elegant design because a single rule — roll or stop — produces a decision that intersects with probability theory, risk management, competitive positioning, and psychological pressure simultaneously. Pure chance is transformed into meaningful play through one carefully placed choice.

Breakdowns in Uncertainty

The chapter closes by examining three ways that the operation of probability can "break down" in games — not as failures, but as challenges and opportunities for designers to understand.

Breakdown 1: Computer Randomness

Digital games rely heavily on random number generators (RNGs) — but computers cannot generate truly random numbers. They execute deterministic algorithms (such as the logistic rule: choose a starting number, multiply by 4, subtract 4 times the square of the starting number) that produce sequences which appear random but are completely determined by the starting value (the seed).

This matters in practice. Eric Zimmerman describes a game prototype featuring microbe-like creatures in a simulated fluid environment. The microbes exhibited complex behavior, but one aspect was puzzling: they always drifted toward the upper-left corner of their 2D environment. After extensive debugging, the cause was found: a programming error in the randomizing function caused the upper-left corner to be counted twice, giving it double the probability of any other position. Even this small bias — tiny in any individual step — compounded through complex system interactions to produce a strong systematic drift.

The lesson: game designers working with digital games must understand how their game's random number generation works. Bugs in randomization functions can produce systematic biases that operate invisibly until they ruin the play experience.

Breakdown 2: Strategizing Chance

Players sometimes transform chance mechanisms into strategic activities without the designer's knowledge or intention. Folklorist Kenneth Goldstein studied children aged 4–14 in northwest Philadelphia in 1967, observing "counting-out" games — the eenie-meenie-miny-moe style procedures used to select who is "It" for playground games like Kick the Can.

Children described counting-out as a purely random procedure — a game of chance that fairly selected a player. But Goldstein's analysis revealed that experienced children had developed six systematic methods for manipulating the outcome:

Specific Rhyme Repertory — Selecting a rhyme of specific length to land on a desired player.
Extension of Rhyme — Adding phrases mid-rhyme to shift the landing point away from an undesired outcome.
Skipping Regular Counts — Quietly skipping yourself when the count would land on you; the most obvious and least socially accepted method.
Stopping or Continuing — Deciding whether the selected player is "counted out" or merely first-selected, changing the game's interpretation after the fact.
Changing Positions — Subtly shifting position in the circle to change where the rhyme will end.
Respite by Calling Out — Calling "safe" or "free" to exempt yourself from counting in the current round.

The paradox: a game players describe as pure chance turns out to be rich with strategic complexity for experienced players. The design lesson is not that chance should be protected from strategic manipulation, but that designers should anticipate when and how players will attempt to control "random" elements — because they always will. This can be the starting point for deliberate design: build games where players can legally construct or modify the "random" component.

Breakdown 3: Probability Fallacies

Game players and the general public hold systematic misunderstandings about probability. From Epstein's catalogue, the authors list the most common:

Overvaluing the Long Shot
Players overvalue low-probability, high-gain bets relative to high-probability, low-gain bets of equivalent expected value. The lottery is the classic case.
Additive Fallacy
Treating successive chance events as additive. The chance of rolling a 1 on one die is 1/6 ≈ 16.67%. The chance of rolling a 1 on at least one of two dice is not 2/6 ≈ 33.33% — it is 11/36 ≈ 30.56%. Successive probabilities compound, not add.
The Monte Carlo Syndrome
Believing that after a run of failures, a success is "due." If the Roulette wheel has landed on black five times in a row, it is not more likely to land on red — each spin is independent. Past outcomes do not affect future probabilities.
Overemphasis on Good Outcomes
Overestimating the probability of positive low-probability events while underestimating equivalent-probability negative ones. Winning the lottery and dying in a car accident the next day are roughly equally likely — but feel very different.
Lightning Striking Twice
Believing that highly unlikely negative events won't repeat (struck by lightning twice) while highly unlikely positive events will (winning the jackpot again). In both cases, past occurrence has no effect on future probability.
Luck
From a purely mathematical standpoint, luck does not exist. People aren't lucky; dice aren't lucky; dates aren't lucky. But widespread belief in luck persists even among experienced game players — a fact with significant design implications.

Each fallacy is a design opportunity as well as a pitfall. The long-shot fallacy explains why players overvalue lottery-style mechanics — building this into a game intentionally can create powerful engagement. The Monte Carlo Syndrome can be exploited to create games where players feel momentum is building even when the underlying probability is flat. Designing with fallacies in mind — rather than against them — is a sophisticated design move.

Meaningful Chance

But chance in a game is never sure. That's what makes the game interesting. Not only is there a possibility that, despite the odds against us, the chance we take will pay off. There is also the further possibility that, despite the apparent confidence of the players, this hand, which seems to be of markedly unimpressive value, might be, in fact, the best of all. My two kings might win the game for me. That's what confidence games are all about. They provide the opportunity and reward for your display of self as well as for your ability to play well with chance — they call for control over yourself as well as control over the game. — Bernie DeKoven, The Well-Played Game

The chapter closes by returning to the relationship between formal probability and player experience. The purely mathematical functioning of uncertainty is necessary but not sufficient to understand what chance does in a game. As DeKoven observes, a player interacts with the system of a game — taking risks, placing bets, calculating odds. But the system is also interacting with the player, making demands, rewarding and punishing, asking for leaps of faith.

Formal game systems and the players who inhabit them cannot be separated. The meaning of chance in a game is not in the probability distribution — it is in the relationship between the player and the uncertainty. The risk is personal. The hope is personal. The sting of a bad roll and the thrill of an unlikely success are not mathematical events; they are experiences.

Uncertainty is in the eye of the beholder, or perhaps, in the play of the player.

Chapter Summary

  • Uncertainty is essential to meaningful play. A game with a predetermined outcome cannot support meaningful decisions. Uncertainty operates at both macro-level (overall game outcome) and micro-level (individual moments and events).
  • Three degrees of outcome uncertainty (Epstein): Certainty (fully predetermined), Risk (unknown outcome, known probability), and Uncertainty (unknown outcome, unknown probability). Most games combine all three.
  • The feeling of randomness is a design resource independent of actual randomness. Strategic complexity (Chinese Checkers), social complexity (SiSSYFiGHT), and information asymmetry can all produce a feeling of randomness without any probabilistic mechanism.
  • Dice probability: two-dice sums follow a triangular distribution, with 7 most likely (6/36). This distribution shapes the entire macro-structure of games like Monopoly.
  • Case Study: Thunderstorm — a game of pure chance that produces rich dramatic engagement through the careful formal design of its uncertainty structure (dice reduction cycles, house-building progression).
  • Case Study: Pig — a single choice (roll or stop) transforms pure chance into meaningful play by placing risk management, probability calculation, and competitive positioning at the nexus of every decision.
  • Three breakdowns of probability: Computer randomness is deterministic (understand your RNG); players strategize chance (anticipate manipulation and design for it); probability fallacies are real and can be exploited positively in design.
  • The experience of chance is personal, not mathematical. Uncertainty is in the play of the player — it exists at the intersection of formal probability and human meaning-making.
