Rules of Play — Katie Salen & Eric Zimmerman · MIT Press, 2004

Chapter 2

The Design Process

Overview

Chapter 2 grounds the book's theoretical ambitions in practice. Where Chapter 1 mapped the landscape of game studies and established a critical vocabulary, this chapter focuses on how games actually come into being — the messy, iterative, deeply human work of design. The authors describe their own approach to game design, present four games commissioned for the book, and offer a structured set of design exercises. The chapter closes with a commissioned essay by Reiner Knizia on the development of The Lord of the Rings board game.

The central claim: game design is not solved through planning — it is discovered through play.

Iterative Design

Salen and Zimmerman advocate a play-centered, iterative design methodology. The designer's role is not to engineer a perfect system on paper but to prototype, play, observe, and refine — repeatedly, until the game becomes what it needs to be.

Design is a process of discovery. You cannot know the full shape of a game until you have played it. Each prototype is a question; each playtest session is an answer — and usually a new question. — paraphrase of the chapter's core position

The process is cyclic, not linear:

01

Prototype

02

Playtest

03

Evaluate

04

Refine

A key heuristic: the first playable prototype should exist no later than 20% into the project schedule. Leaving playtesting until late in development is a common and costly mistake — it forecloses the most important feedback loop precisely when there is still time to act on it.

This approach requires embracing failure as information. A broken prototype is not a setback; it is a data point. The designer who plays their own game early and often will always outperform the designer who waits for it to be "ready."

The Commissions

To demonstrate design in action, Salen and Zimmerman commissioned four original games from established designers. Each game illustrates different design approaches, player structures, and thematic concerns. All four are documented and analyzed throughout the book.

Sibling Rivalry Richard Garfield · Board game · 2+ players

A competitive board game centered on conflict between siblings, using dice and tracks. Garfield — creator of Magic: The Gathering — brings deep experience with card and probability mechanics to a game about familial power dynamics. The design explores escalation, negotiation, and the particular cruelty of sibling competition.

Ironclad Frank Lantz · 2 players

A two-player game built around two simultaneous sub-games: arena combat and philosophical debate. Lantz's design is formally ambitious — the player must manage strategic action and argument at the same time, creating an unusual cognitive double-bind. Ironclad raises questions about what counts as "winning" when both physical and intellectual contest are on the table.

Sneak Kira Snyder · Social deception · 4+ players

A social deception game in which players must identify a hidden double agent among them. Sneak belongs to the tradition of games like Werewolf and Mafia — its mechanics are primarily social, and the "game system" is largely constructed through player behavior, accusation, and trust. The design examines how games can transform everyday social dynamics into structured play.

Caribbean Star James Ernest · Card game · 2 players

A card game in which two cruise ship magicians compete for passengers and prestige. Ernest, founder of Cheapass Games, brings his characteristic wit and economy to a design that uses a standard deck of cards to create a competitive performance system. The game explores themes of spectacle, rivalry, and the economics of entertainment.

Further Reading

The Well-Played Game Bernard DeKoven

A foundational text on play culture, arguing that the quality of play matters more than any formal game structure. DeKoven's concept of the "well-played game" — one in which all players are fully engaged and the experience is genuinely shared — is a touchstone for thinking about what games are actually for.

The New Games Book Andrew Fluegelman & Shoshana Tembeck (eds.)

A collection of cooperative and non-competitive games emerging from the New Games movement of the 1970s. Challenges the assumption that games must be zero-sum, and offers practical designs built around play-for-play's-sake rather than winning. Influential on thinking about social game design and community play.

New Rules for Classic Games R. Wayne Schmittberger

A systematic survey of rule variations for well-known games — chess, checkers, backgammon, and dozens more. An invaluable resource for modification exercises: seeing how small rule changes ripple through a game's entire system makes the structural logic of design visible in ways that creation alone cannot.

Commissioned Essay
Designing Lord of the Rings:
The Fellowship of the Ring
Reiner Knizia

Design Process

Knizia opens by noting that he has no fixed design process — and that this is intentional. The right approach depends entirely on the game: its source material, its intended experience, its audience. What remains constant are four fundamental questions that any game design must answer.

Who am I?
What role does the player occupy in the game world? What is the player's relationship to the material?
What am I trying to achieve?
What is the win condition — and more importantly, what does winning feel like? What experience is the goal state meant to produce?
What are my main choices?
What decisions does the player actually make? Where does agency live in the design?
How do I win?
What is the path from opening state to victory? How does the game's system reward good play?

For Lord of the Rings, these questions had a specific shape: players would be hobbits, collectively trying to destroy the One Ring before Sauron's forces overwhelmed them. The game needed to be cooperative — the source material demanded it. Tolkien's story is not a competitive one.

Knizia describes starting from feel, world, and system rather than from mechanics. The emotional experience he wanted to produce — fellowship under pressure, sacrifice, the weight of the Ring — had to be built backwards into a rule structure that could reliably generate it.

The Scripted Game System

The core structural innovation of the design is what Knizia calls the "scripted game system" — a method for distilling the key arc of a narrative into a playable sequence of mechanical events.

The design uses two types of boards. A central "summary board" tracks the overall progress of the fellowship — how close they are to Mount Doom, how corrupted the Ring-bearer has become. Individual "adventure boards" represent specific story locations: Bag End, Moria, Helm's Deep, Shelob's Lair, Mount Doom. Each adventure board scripts the events of that location, making the narrative structure into a mechanical structure.

The corruption line was the heart of the design. Everything else in the game exists in relationship to it — pushing players toward decisions that feel morally weighted, not just strategically optimal. — Reiner Knizia

The corruption track — measuring the Ring-bearer's susceptibility to the Ring — creates the game's central tension. Players must balance the drive to complete each adventure board against the constant pressure of corruption. An event deck on each board creates time pressure: the pile of cards will run out, and if the players haven't completed the board's objectives before it does, they lose ground they cannot recover.

This system produces a game that feels scripted without being predetermined. Players follow Tolkien's path but face genuine decisions at every step about how to allocate resources, when to sacrifice, and how much corruption to absorb.

Playtesting

Knizia's playtesting methodology is disciplined and systematic. He begins with mental play — working through the game's logic in his head before building any physical components. Only once the system holds together conceptually does he construct a physical prototype.

Before each session, he plans exactly which issues he wants to observe. He does not play a session and wait to see what happens; he enters with specific hypotheses and watches for specific evidence. After each session he records what he found.

The schedule was intensive: three to four sessions per week. The first stage of playtesting used only a single scenario (Bag End), not the full game. This allowed Knizia to stress-test the core mechanics before adding the complexity of the full campaign. Once Bag End worked, he added Moria. Then Helm's Deep. Then Shelob's Lair. Then Mount Doom. Each location was proven independently before being integrated into the whole.

This staged approach mirrors the chapter's broader argument about iterative design. Knizia did not attempt to test a complete, five-board game with all its moving parts simultaneously. He built and tested in layers, which let him understand which problems belonged to which part of the system.

More Changes

Knizia describes beginning with more features than the final game contains — a deliberate strategy. The first prototype was deliberately over-designed: too many card types, too many special rules, too many mechanical variables. The playtesting process became a process of elimination, cutting everything that didn't earn its place.

Two specific design problems illuminate his approach:

Balancing for player count

The game had to work for two, three, four, or five players without becoming either trivially easy or impossibly hard. Player count changes resource distribution, decision complexity, and collective communication dynamics in ways that cannot be solved with a single formula. Knizia iterated separately on each player-count configuration, treating each as a distinct design problem that happened to share a rule set.

The Gandalf solution

A particularly elegant example of solving two problems at once: the Gandalf cards were designed simultaneously to give players meaningful tactical choices and to give shields — the game's primary resource — a clear purpose and identity. Rather than addressing each problem with a separate mechanism, Knizia found a single design element that resolved both. This kind of economy — one solution for multiple problems — is a mark of mature design.

Target audience calibration was another significant challenge. The game needed to be accessible enough for family play while retaining enough depth for dedicated hobbyists. These requirements pull in opposite directions: accessibility demands simplicity, depth demands complexity. Knizia resolved this partly through the Tolkien license itself — the audience's emotional investment in the source material provided the motivation to engage with more complex mechanics than a purely abstract game could demand.

The Road Goes Ever On

Development on Lord of the Rings lasted eighteen months. The game was released in October 2000.

October 2000

Base game released.

2001

Friends & Foes expansion released, adding new challenges and a Sauron player variant.

2002

Sauron expansion released, formalizing the one-versus-many structure.

By 2004

Over one million copies sold in seventeen languages.

The commercial and critical success of the game validated the design approach. But Knizia's account is not triumphalist — he is careful to describe what was cut, what was reconsidered, and where the design surprised him. The essay reads as an honest account of a process that was often uncertain, not a retrospective justification of decisions that were always obviously correct.

A game is not finished when you have added everything. It is finished when you can take nothing more away. — paraphrase of Knizia's design philosophy
About the Author

Reiner Knizia was born in Germany and lives in the United Kingdom. He holds a doctorate in Mathematics and worked as Director of Strategic Planning at major German banks before designing games full-time. At the time of publication, he had over 200 published games to his name — among the most prolific and commercially successful game designers in the world.
