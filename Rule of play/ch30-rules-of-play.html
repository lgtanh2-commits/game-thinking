Unit 4: Culture  ·  Chapter 30

Games as Cultural Rhetoric

Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Salen & Zimmerman

"Soccer is the most flexible of political tools: it fits any ideology, turns its colors to any regime, waves for anyone who asks it to." — Simon Kuper, Football Against the Enemy

Introducing Cultural Rhetoric

Soccer's extraordinary malleability across political contexts — played with equal passion under fascism and socialism, democracy and monarchy — illustrates the central problem of this chapter. Games are not culturally neutral. They carry meanings that serve particular ideological purposes. But which meanings? And whose purposes?

The schema Games as Cultural Rhetoric examines how games embody, express, and reinforce cultural values. The term "rhetoric" is used here in a broad sense. Game theorist Brian Sutton-Smith defines play rhetoric as "a form of persuasive discourse, or implicit cultural narrative." A cultural rhetoric is not necessarily an explicit argument — it is a set of values and assumptions built into the form and content of cultural practice, shaping how that practice is understood and what purposes it serves.

Games participate in cultural rhetoric in ways that are often invisible. The rules, themes, player roles, and win conditions of a game all implicitly argue for particular ways of understanding competition, cooperation, gender, race, power, and play itself.

Sutton-Smith's Seven Rhetorics of Play

Based on decades of research, Brian Sutton-Smith identified seven major cultural rhetorics of play — the dominant frames through which different cultures and historical periods have made sense of what play is and why it matters.

1. Play as Progress (Contemporary) The dominant rhetoric in modern Western culture: play is valuable because it develops children into competent adults. Play teaches, trains, and prepares. Most educational game design operates from this rhetoric.
2. Play as Fate (Ancient) Play is controlled by destiny, luck, or divine will. Gambling and games of chance are the paradigm cases. The player submits to forces beyond rational control — gods, spirits, or fortune.
3. Play as Power (Ancient) Play enacts competition, struggle, and the domination of opponents. Sports and athletic contests are the primary examples. The rhetoric of power is central to mainstream competitive game design.
4. Play as Identity (Ancient) Play reinforces the identity and solidarity of a community. Festivals, rituals, and communal games create and sustain a sense of collective belonging. Play mirrors and performs social identity.
5. Play as the Imaginary (Contemporary) Play as art, creativity, and improvisation. This rhetoric values play for its capacity to produce novel experiences and aesthetic pleasure. Avant-garde game design and art games often operate here.
6. Play as the Self (Contemporary) Individualistic play — hobbies, solitary games, extreme sports, personal challenge. This rhetoric frames play as self-expression and self-development, disconnected from community or utility.
7. Play as Frivolity (Ancient) The rhetoric of the Trickster and the Fool — play as opposition to serious work, as nonsense, inversion, and comic subversion. Often the rhetoric adopted by those who resist dominant cultural norms.

No single rhetoric dominates all games. Most games participate in multiple rhetorics simultaneously, and the same game can be framed differently by different communities and at different historical moments. Understanding which rhetorics a game draws on — and which it suppresses — is a key tool for cultural analysis.

A Tale of Two Games: Landlord vs. Monopoly

Perhaps the most striking case study in games as cultural rhetoric is the transformation of a single game design through opposing ideological purposes.

The Landlord's Game (1904) Designed by Lizzie Magie, a follower of economist Henry George. The game was meant to demonstrate the injustice of land monopoly and illustrate George's "single-tax" theory. Players could see exactly how landlords extracted wealth from tenants without adding value. The game's rhetoric was explicitly anti-capitalist: property ownership as systemic exploitation. Its rules were open-licensed so anyone could copy and improve them.
Monopoly (1935) The same game mechanics, rebranded and sold by Parker Brothers. The cultural rhetoric reversed completely: now the game celebrated the accumulation of property and the elimination of opponents through economic dominance. Winning through ruthless real-estate acquisition became fun rather than a critique. The game's rhetoric was pro-capitalist: property ownership as triumph and aspiration.
"Same formal system. Opposite cultural rhetoric. The rules had not changed — the cultural frame had." — Salen & Zimmerman

This case illustrates a fundamental principle: the rhetoric of a game is not merely contained in its formal mechanics but in the cultural framing — the theme, the setting, the win conditions, the context of distribution — that surrounds those mechanics. Game designers can use the same formal system to argue for radically different cultural values.

Vampire: The Masquerade and Play as Identity

Vampire: The Masquerade, the tabletop role-playing game published in 1991, drew in players from Goth subculture — a community defined by its embrace of darkness, transgression, and aesthetic resistance to mainstream culture. The game offered a system in which the rhetoric of play as identity was dominant: playing a vampire was a way of performing and affirming Goth identity.

In this case, the cultural rhetoric did not emerge from the game's designers — it entered the game from outside, as a community appropriated the game system as a vehicle for its own cultural self-expression. The magic circle became permeable: Goth aesthetics, values, and social practices flowed into the game, transforming its meaning. This is an example of cultural rhetoric operating in the direction from context into game, rather than from game into context.

Rhetorics of Gender

Gender provides some of the most vivid examples of cultural rhetoric at work in games. Game researcher Henry Jenkins analyzed the spatial rhetoric of "boy games" in the 1990s — games built around adventure islands, platform scrollers, and territory-conquest mechanics that encoded specifically masculine fantasies of freedom, exploration, and combat.

Lara Croft

The introduction of Lara Croft in Tomb Raider (1996) represented a complex moment in the rhetoric of game gender. On one level, she offered a female protagonist in action games — a welcome novelty. On another, her physique was an extreme fantasy construction clearly designed to appeal to a presumed male audience. The game simultaneously opened a space for female identification and reproduced the male gaze, creating a rhetoric of gender that was contradictory and contested.

Ms. Pac-Man and Samus Aran

Ms. Pac-Man added a bow and lipstick to Pac-Man, feminizing the protagonist through cosmetic markers rather than structural change. Metroid's Samus Aran performed the opposite maneuver: a game marketed as featuring a male hero that revealed its protagonist to be female only at the end. Both games illuminate how deeply gender is embedded in rhetorical framing — how much of what a character "means" comes from cultural context rather than formal properties.

SiSSYFiGHT 2000

Designed by Word.com in 2000, SiSSYFiGHT 2000 is a multiplayer browser game in which elementary school girls compete for social dominance through teasing, tattling, scratching, and licking lollipops. The game deliberately adopts and interrogates the rhetoric of female social aggression. By making the normally invisible politics of schoolgirl social life into explicit game mechanics, it creates a space of cultural critique through play — a rhetoric of gender made visible through formalization.

Transforming Spaces: The New Games Movement

The New Games Movement of the 1970s represented a deliberate attempt to redesign the cultural rhetoric of competitive sport. Founded in the aftermath of the Vietnam War and the counterculture movements of the 1960s, the movement sought to create games that embodied values of cooperation, inclusion, and nonviolence.

The movement's slogan — "Play hard. Play fair. Nobody hurt." — announced its rhetorical agenda. Games like Earthball, in which hundreds of players cooperate to keep an enormous ball in motion, transformed the rhetoric of play as power into the rhetoric of play as identity and imaginary. The formal structures of competition (sides, victory conditions) were replaced with collective improvisation.

"The New Games Movement made the cultural rhetoric of its games explicit, treating game design as a form of cultural activism. Play hard. Play fair. Nobody hurt." — Salen & Zimmerman

Battling Toys: Design as Cultural Argument

An installation game created by Eric Zimmerman at the Bellevue Art Museum in 2000 demonstrates how game design can engage cultural rhetoric directly. The game — "The [adjective] toy beats the [adjective] toy" — invited players to adjudicate conflicts between cultural artifacts (toys representing different aspects of consumer culture) by assigning descriptors that expressed value judgments. Play became an explicit occasion for making cultural arguments, revealing how deeply value-laden the objects of everyday life are.

The game's structure exposed the rhetoric embedded in consumer culture: which toys are "powerful," which are "weak," which associations seem natural and which seem absurd. By formalizing cultural judgment into a game system, it made visible the usually invisible rhetorical work that culture performs.

Key Concepts

  • A cultural rhetoric is an implicit cultural narrative or persuasive framework embedded in cultural practices, including games.
  • Sutton-Smith identified seven rhetorics of play: Progress, Fate, Power, Identity, the Imaginary, the Self, and Frivolity — each framing the value and purpose of play differently.
  • The Landlord's Game vs. Monopoly demonstrates that the same formal system can carry opposite cultural rhetorics depending on framing and context.
  • Cultural rhetoric flows in both directions: game designers embed values in game structures, and player communities project their own cultural values onto games.
  • Gender rhetoric is particularly visible in games, through character design, spatial logic, and the framing of player roles.
  • Games can deploy cultural rhetoric critically, using formal structures to make cultural values visible and available for reflection.
