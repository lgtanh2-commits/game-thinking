Games as Cybernetic Systems

Games are self-regulating systems. Feedback loops built into the rules determine whether a game stays balanced, spirals toward a winner, or snaps shut.

Cybernetics and Control

Cybernetics is the science of control and communication in animals and machines — a term coined by mathematician Norbert Wiener in his landmark 1948 work Cybernetics, or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine. Though the word sounds technical and intimidating, the core idea is elegant: cybernetics studies how systems regulate themselves by processing information about their own behavior.

A cybernetic system has three essential components working in a loop:

Sensor
Measures the current state of the system relative to some goal or standard. It monitors what is actually happening.
Comparator
Evaluates the difference between the current state (what the sensor reports) and the desired state (the goal). It determines whether a corrective response is needed.
Activator
Takes action to reduce the gap between current state and desired state. It changes the behavior of the system in response to what the comparator has determined.

The classic example is a thermostat. The sensor measures room temperature; the comparator determines whether the temperature is above or below the desired setting; the activator (the heating or cooling system) responds accordingly. The thermostat doesn't just turn on and stay on — it regulates based on continuous feedback about the current state of the system.

Games are full of cybernetic loops. Every time a rule causes the game state to adjust in response to player actions — slowing down a leader, helping a trailing player, triggering a catching-up mechanism — a cybernetic system is at work. Understanding the feedback structure of a game is one of the most powerful tools a game designer can have.

Negative and Positive Feedback

Cybernetics distinguishes two fundamental types of feedback loops, and both appear constantly in games:

Negative Feedback A negative feedback loop works against a deviation from the norm — it is a corrective force. When one player gets ahead, negative feedback mechanisms kick in to slow them down or help trailing players catch up. The effect is stabilizing: it tends to keep the game balanced and competitive. In racing games, "rubber-banding" AI is a deliberate negative feedback system.
Positive Feedback A positive feedback loop amplifies a deviation from the norm — it snowballs. When one player gets ahead, positive feedback makes them even more likely to stay ahead and pull further away. The effect is destabilizing: early advantages compound, the game's outcome becomes increasingly determined. Monopoly's property accumulation is a positive feedback loop.

Neither type is inherently good or bad as a design element — each serves different purposes and creates different player experiences. Negative feedback keeps competitions close and creates dramatic tension. Positive feedback creates a satisfying sense of momentum and reward for early skill. The question for designers is always: what kind of experience do you want the feedback structure to produce?

LeBlanc's Design Framework

Game designer Marc LeBlanc has developed a framework for applying feedback loop analysis directly to game design. His model maps the cybernetic concepts of sensor, comparator, and activator onto game systems, asking designers to identify precisely how information about the game state is detected, evaluated, and acted upon within the rules.

LeBlanc's model helps designers identify feedback structures that are built deliberately into a game — and, equally importantly, those that emerge by accident without the designer realizing it. A feedback loop that isn't consciously designed can easily undermine the experience the designer intended.

LeBlanc proposes five design rules for working with feedback systems:

  1. Negative feedback stabilizes the game; positive feedback destabilizes it. Negative feedback keeps the competition close and competitive, correcting imbalances. Positive feedback amplifies differences, pushing the game toward resolution — or runaway victory.
  2. Negative feedback can prolong the game; positive feedback can end it. A game with strong negative feedback loops will tend to stay balanced longer, extending the duration. Positive feedback loops accelerate the endgame: once one player has a significant advantage, the game moves quickly toward conclusion.
  3. Positive feedback magnifies early successes; negative feedback magnifies late ones. In a game dominated by positive feedback, early skill or luck is rewarded disproportionately. In a game with strong negative feedback, the most important moments tend to come at the end, when catching-up mechanisms can no longer forestall the inevitable.
  4. Feedback systems can emerge "by accident" — identify them. Not every feedback loop in a game is designed intentionally. When designers analyze their games, they often discover feedback loops — both positive and negative — that emerged from the interaction of rules never intended to produce them. Identifying these accidental structures is as important as designing deliberate ones.
  5. Feedback systems can take control away from players. A very powerful feedback loop can override player decisions entirely. If positive feedback produces a runaway leader situation early in the game, the other players may feel their choices no longer matter. If negative feedback keeps the game so balanced that no one can ever gain a meaningful advantage, strategic decisions feel meaningless. Good design keeps feedback strong enough to shape play without removing player agency.

Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment

One of the most sophisticated applications of cybernetic principles in game design is Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) — a system in which a game automatically calibrates its level of challenge to match the current ability of the player. DDA is a direct application of negative feedback: when the player is succeeding too easily, the game increases difficulty; when the player is struggling, the game eases up.

Many single-player video games implement DDA systems, often invisibly. Enemy AI becomes more or less aggressive. Item drops become more or less generous. Level layouts adjust subtly. The game is continuously reading the player's performance (the sensor function), evaluating whether the challenge is appropriate (the comparator function), and adjusting its own parameters (the activator function) to keep the player in what game designers sometimes call the "flow channel" — the sweet spot between boredom and frustration.

DDA represents an interesting design choice with trade-offs. On the positive side, it ensures that players of varying skill levels can have a satisfying experience with the same game. On the negative side, it can feel manipulative if players notice it — the sense that the game is "cheating" to keep them engaged rather than responding fairly to their actual skill.

A Simple Die Roll: Chutes and Ladders

Chutes and Ladders is a game of pure chance — but even within its minimal formal structure, cybernetic analysis reveals something interesting. The chutes and ladders themselves are dramatic game elements, but they are not cybernetic: they do not respond to the state of the game. A chute sends any player back regardless of whether they are in first place or last.

However, one rule in Chutes and Ladders is cybernetic: in many versions of the game, a player must roll the exact number needed to land on the final square. If a player overshoots, they must wait for another turn. This rule functions as a mild negative feedback mechanism near the end of the game, because the leading player is more likely to overshoot — they have fewer spaces remaining, and any roll that exceeds the required number is wasted. Trailing players can still roll any number freely, giving them more chances to advance. The exact-landing rule subtly levels the playing field just as the game approaches its conclusion.

This example illustrates a key point: cybernetic structures can be embedded in very simple rules, and identifying them requires careful analysis of how the rules respond to the game state over time — not just how they operate on any single turn.

First-Order and Second-Order Cybernetics

The original cybernetics — sensor, comparator, activator — is sometimes called first-order cybernetics. It assumes an objective outside observer who can measure the system's state from a neutral vantage point. In the 1970s and beyond, theorists began to question this assumption: what happens when the observer is part of the system being observed?

This question gave rise to second-order cybernetics, sometimes called the cybernetics of cybernetics. As N. Katherine Hayles describes in How We Became Posthuman, second-order cybernetics recognizes that the observer cannot stand outside the system — that observation itself shapes the system being observed. In games, this is a real phenomenon. Players are not outside the game system observing it neutrally; they are inside it, their decisions shaping the system's behavior even as the system's feedback loops shape their decisions. The game and the player co-regulate each other.

For game designers, this suggests that the player's experience of a feedback system is as important as the system's objective structure. A negative feedback loop that players perceive as unfair — even if it is mathematically balanced — may undermine the experience. The subjective dimension of cybernetic design matters as much as its formal mechanics.

Chapter Summary

  • Cybernetics is the science of control and communication in self-regulating systems. A cybernetic system has three components: a sensor (monitors current state), a comparator (evaluates the gap between current and desired state), and an activator (takes corrective action).
  • Negative feedback corrects deviations from a norm — it is a stabilizing force that keeps the game balanced and competitive. Positive feedback amplifies deviations — it is a destabilizing force that rewards early advantages and drives the game toward a conclusion.
  • Marc LeBlanc's design rules: negative feedback stabilizes and prolongs; positive feedback destabilizes and ends; positive feedback magnifies early success, negative magnifies late outcomes; feedback loops can emerge by accident; and powerful feedback loops can remove player agency.
  • Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) is an application of negative feedback in which a game automatically calibrates its challenge level to match a player's current ability, keeping play in the "flow channel" between boredom and frustration.
  • Even simple games like Chutes and Ladders contain cybernetic structures: the exact-landing-on-final-space rule functions as a mild negative feedback mechanism that levels the field near the end of the game — even though the chutes and ladders themselves are not cybernetic.
  • Second-order cybernetics recognizes that the observer cannot stand outside the system being observed — in games, players are part of the cybernetic loop, co-regulating the game even as the game regulates them. The player's perceived experience of feedback matters as much as its formal mechanics.
