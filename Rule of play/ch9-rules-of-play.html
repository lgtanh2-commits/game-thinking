Chapter 9 The Magic Circle

This is the problem of the way we get into and out of the play or game… what are the codes which govern these entries and exits? — Brian Sutton-Smith, Child's Play

Entering the System

What does it mean to enter the system of a game? How is it that play begins and ends? What makes up the boundary of a game? These are the questions Chapter 9 addresses — the last set of key concepts before Unit 1 closes.

The concept of artificial conflict is already embedded in the definition of games from Chapter 7. The chapter now unpacks the "artificial" component: the idea that games create their own time and space, separate from ordinary life. Steve Sniderman, in his essay "The Life of Games," notes that the codes governing entry into a game lack explicit representation. Players and fans of any game develop an acute awareness of the game's frame or context, but would be hard pressed to explain in writing exactly what the signs of game-entry are. Even an umpire's yelling "Play Ball!" is not the exact moment the game starts. Players must rely on intuition and cultural experience to know when a game has begun — and during play, they are constantly monitoring the frame, noticing if the conditions for play are still being met.

Psychologist Michael Apter calls this a protective frame: an enchanted zone in which you are confident that no harm can come. Although this frame is psychological, it often has perceptible physical representation — the proscenium arch of the theater, the railings around the park, the boundary line on the cricket pitch. It may also be entirely abstract, constituted solely by the rules governing the game being played.

Salen and Zimmerman name this frame the magic circle — a concept inspired by Huizinga's work on play.

Boundaries

Games take place within set boundaries established by the act of play. This is not a metaphor — there really is a distinct boundary. Consider the contrast between a toy and a game.

Toy (doll)

A child can slowly and gradually enter into a play relationship with a doll — glancing at it, picking it up, setting it down, dragging it around. The boundary between playing with the doll and not playing with it is fuzzy and permeable. There is no formal beginning, no defined end, no quantifiable outcome.

Game (Tic-Tac-Toe)

To play Tic-Tac-Toe, the children must gather materials, draw the grid, and follow proper rules each turn as they progress. The activity is richly formalized — beginning, middle, quantifiable outcome. Either they are playing or they are not. There is no ambiguity.

The same analysis applies in digital contexts. A user's casual interaction with a toy-like screensaver — wiggling the mouse to make patterns — is informal and unbound by rules. A game of Tetris provides a formalized boundary regarding play: the game is either in play or it is not. A player who pauses Tetris is stepping out of the game space, formally suspending the magic circle before stepping back in — just as two Tennis players who stop for a drink of water pause the game rather than casually exit it.

Naming the Magic Circle

The term "magic circle" comes from a passage in Huizinga's Homo Ludens:

All play moves and has its being within a play-ground marked off beforehand either materially or ideally, deliberately or as a matter of course…. The arena, the card-table, the magic circle, the temple, the stage, the screen, the tennis court, the court of justice, etc., are all in form and function play-grounds, i.e., forbidden spots, isolated, hedged round, hallowed, within which special rules obtain. All are temporary worlds within the ordinary world, dedicated to the performance of an act apart. — Johan Huizinga, Homo Ludens (1938)

The magic circle is just one item in Huizinga's list of play-grounds, but Salen and Zimmerman use it as shorthand for the broader idea: a special place in time and space created by a game. The magic circle is both a spatial concept and a temporal one. As a closed circle, it circumscribes a space enclosed and separate from the real world. As a marker of time, it is like a clock — it represents a path with a beginning and an end, but one that is also endlessly repeatable, without beginning and end. The magic circle inscribes a space that is repeatable, simultaneously limited and limitless: a finite space with infinite possibility.

Enter In

To play a game means entering into a magic circle, or perhaps creating one as the game begins. The magic circle may have a physical component — the board of a board game, the playing field of an athletic contest — or it may exist in no particular physical form at all. Arm wrestling, for example, requires no special space or material: the game simply begins when players decide to play.

Within the magic circle, special meanings accrue and cluster around objects and behaviors. A new reality is created, defined by the rules of the game and inhabited by its players. Before a game of Chutes and Ladders starts, there is just a board, some plastic pieces, and a die. But once the game begins, everything changes. Suddenly, the materials represent something specific. This plastic token is you. These rules tell you how to roll the die and move. It matters, very much, which plastic token reaches the end first.

The Hide-and-Seek example makes this transformation vivid. A group of kids in a suburban front yard, casually talking, decide to play. One takes a rock and plants it in the middle of the yard to represent home base. Someone is chosen as "It." They scatter and hide. All at once, the relationships among the players have taken on special meanings. Who is "It" and who is not? Who is hidden and who can be seen? Who is captured and who is free? The magic circle of the game is the boundary of this game space — and within this boundary, the rules of the game play out and have authority.

Temporary Worlds

How permeable is the boundary between the game world and the real world? Huizinga calls play-worlds "temporary worlds within the ordinary world." This framing implies the magic circle is neither completely sealed off from nor identical with ordinary reality. The question of how open or closed the magic circle is depends on which schema is used to analyze it.

Rules

Games considered as RULES are closed systems. The formal rules exist prior to and independent of any actual player — the rules of chess are the rules of chess regardless of who plays.

Play

Games considered as PLAY can be either closed or open. Players bring in their expectations, likes, social relationships. It is impossible to ignore the fact that games are open, a reflection of the players who play them.

Culture

Games considered as CULTURE are extremely open systems. The internal functioning of the game is not emphasized; instead, the focus is on how the game exchanges meaning with culture at large.

Bernard DeKoven puts it well in The Well-Played Game: "Boundaries help separate the game from life. They have a critical function in maintaining the fiction of the game so that the aspects of reality with which we do not choose to play can be left safely out-side." The answer to whether games are open or closed systems depends entirely on which schema we use to understand them.

The Lusory Attitude

The magic circle creates a specific psychological challenge. To decide to sit down and play Monopoly is a real commitment — you cannot simply quit in the middle without disrupting the game and upsetting other players. What psychological attitude is required of a player entering a game?

Bernard Suits provides the answer through examples. If the goal of boxing is to make the other fighter stay down for a count of ten, the most efficient means would be to shoot the opponent. Boxers instead put on padded gloves and strike only in limited, stylized ways. If the goal of golf is to get a small ball into a hole in the ground as efficiently as possible, the most natural means would be to pick it up and place it there. Instead, golfers walk three or four hundred yards away and propel the ball with a stick. Suits writes:

In anything but a game the gratuitous introduction of unnecessary obstacles to the achievement of an end is regarded as a decidedly irrational thing to do, whereas in games it appears to be an absolutely essential thing to do. — Bernard Suits, The Grasshopper: Games, Life, and Utopia

Suits calls this state of mind the lusory attitude — the willingness to adopt rules that require one to employ worse rather than better means for reaching an end. The word "ludo" means play in Latin; "lusory" shares its root with "ludens" in Huizinga's Homo Ludens. The lusory attitude is an extremely useful concept because it describes the fundamental psychological posture required of game players.

To play a game is in many ways an act of "faith" — it invests the game with its special meaning. Without willing players, a game is merely a formal system waiting to be inhabited, like a piece of sheet music waiting to be played. The lusory attitude also implies more than mere acceptance of limitations: it means accepting the rules because the play of the game is an end in itself. Players take on the lusory attitude for the pleasure of play itself.

The marble game illustrates this vividly. A cluster of boys could just grab each other's marbles and run. Instead, they chalk a circle on the sidewalk, place a marble inside, and suddenly a game exists that guides and arbitrates their actions. They play for keeps — a dramatic, formal process that may end in winning marbles from opponents or losing one's own collection. There is pleasure in this inefficiency. In Missile Command, the missile slowly climbs from the bottom of the screen rather than instantly appearing at the crosshairs. In bowling, the ball is rolled from a distance rather than carried to the pins. From the gap between action and outcome, in the friction between frustrated desire and a seductive goal, bubbles up the unique enjoyment of game play.

The magic circle is fragile. It requires constant maintenance to remain intact. Over the course of the following chapters, Salen and Zimmerman explore the design structures that serve to create and support the magic circle, as well as the qualities of game design that affect the lusory attitude and the possibility of meaningful play.

Summary

Frame Every game exists within a specially demarcated time and space. The frame communicates to players, consciously or unconsciously, that a game is being played.
Magic circle The space within which a game takes place. The formalized nature of games makes the magic circle explicit, unlike informal play which has no distinct boundary.
Meanings Within the magic circle, the game's rules create a special set of meanings for players. These meanings guide the play of the game.
Open / closed Considered as RULES, a game is a closed system. Considered as PLAY, a game is both open and closed. Considered as CULTURE, a game is open.
Lusory attitude The state of mind required to enter the play of a game. A group of players accepts the limitations of the rules because of the pleasure a game can afford.
