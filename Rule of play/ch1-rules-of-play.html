Rules of Play — Game Design Fundamentals  ·  Katie Salen & Eric Zimmerman  ·  MIT Press, 2004

Chapter 1

What Is This Book About?

Overview

The book covers games of all kinds — paper-based strategy games and first-person shooters, classical board games and glitzy gambling games, math puzzles and professional sports, austere text adventures and giggly teenage party games. The common thread running through all of them is a single conceptual framework: game design.

To understand what this means, we first need to clarify what a game designer is. A game designer is a particular kind of designer — much like a graphic designer, industrial designer, or architect — whose focus is on game play: conceiving and designing rules and structures that result in an experience for players. A game designer is not necessarily a programmer, visual designer, or project manager, though they may play those roles as well.

Game design, as a discipline, requires treating games as objects of study in their own right — not as servants of sociology, literary criticism, or computer science. Because game design is an emerging discipline, it borrows from adjacent fields (mathematics, cognitive science, semiotics, cultural studies), but does so in the service of establishing game design as a field of its own.

"Each person defines games in his own way — the anthropologists and folklorists in terms of historical origins; the military men, businessmen, and educators in terms of usages; the social scientists in terms of psychological and social functions." — E.M. Avedon, The Study of Games

This book is about game design, not game development. It is not a how-to guide for making successful digital games. It does not cover digital game programming, choosing development tools, team dynamics, funding, marketing, or distribution. Instead, it bridges the theoretical and practical aspects of making games — looking closely at games as designed systems, identifying patterns within their complexity, and outlining both the concepts behind meaningful play and concrete methods for implementing those concepts.

Establishing a Critical Discourse

One way to describe the project of this book is to say that it is working to establish a critical discourse for game design. As veteran game designer Warren Spector put it: "It is absolutely vital that we start to build a vocabulary that allows us to examine, with some degree of precision, how games evoke emotional-intellectual responses in players."

Media theorist and game scholar Henry Jenkins identifies four ways that building a critical discourse around games can assist not just game designers, but the field as a whole:

Training
A common language facilitates the education of game designers, letting them explore their medium in more variety and depth.
Generational Transfer
A disciplinary vocabulary lets designers and developers pass on skills and knowledge, rather than solving the same problems over and over in isolation.
Audience-Building
In finding a way to speak about games, games can be reviewed, critiqued, and advertised to the public in more sophisticated ways.
Buffer Against Criticism
A critical discourse gives the field the vocabulary and understanding to defend against attempts to censor or regulate game content.

Part of creating a critical discourse is defining concepts. The key terms that form the foundation of the vocabulary developed in this book are: game, design, interactivity, system, play, and culture. These terms thread their way through multiple and sometimes contradictory contexts.

A definition is not a closed or scientific representation of "reality." For a designer, its value is its ability to serve as a critical tool for understanding and solving design problems. Definitions stake out boundaries — the way a set of points defines a plane in space. The authors acknowledge that their definitions leave some things out and work better in some circumstances than others. But this doesn't lessen their overall utility.

Ways of Looking

"A game is a particular way of looking at something, anything." — Clark C. Abt, Serious Games

Social theorist Clark C. Abt's suggestion is a powerful one. It points to the approach the book takes: how can games be used as a way to understand aesthetics, communication, culture, and other areas of our world? And conversely, how can understanding those areas enrich the practice of designing games?

Game designer and theorist Jesper Juul has noted that theories about games tend to fall into two camps: Everything is a game ("War is a game; politics are a game; life is a game; everything is a game!") or Games are X ("Games are an interactive storytelling medium." "Games are how a child learns about rules."). Both framings are too narrow.

Consider poetry. Saying that poetry is storytelling misses the picture — storytelling is just one of many possible perspectives. You could also look at poetry formally, within the context of rhyme and meter, or historically, with an emphasis on printing technologies. Each perspective offers access to only part of the total picture. Taken together, these frames begin to sketch out the complex cultural phenomenon we call poetry. In Rules of Play, this is exactly what is done with games.

Architect Christopher Alexander, writing about the design of a simple kettle, illustrates how even the most ordinary design problem contains a web of competing requirements — material costs, heat retention, ergonomics, safety, storage, aesthetics. Games share this degree of complexity. As products of human culture, games fulfill a range of needs, desires, pleasures, and uses. As products of design culture, they reflect a host of technological, material, formal, and economic concerns. Viewing such a complex phenomenon from a single perspective would miss most of the design problem entirely. The solution: game design schemas.

Game Design Schemas

A schema is a way of framing and organizing knowledge. A game design schema is a way of understanding games — a conceptual lens that can be applied to the analysis or creation of a game. The schemas employed in this book include looking at games through the mathematical lens of probability, looking at them as contexts for social interaction, looking at them as storytelling systems, and looking at them as sites of cultural resistance.

These varied points of view are organized according to three primary schemas, each containing a cluster of related schemas. The three primary schemas are Rules, Play, and Culture:

Rules

Contains formal game design schemas that focus on the essential logical and mathematical structures of a game.

Play

Contains experiential, social, and representational schemas that foreground the player's participation with the game and with other players.

Culture

Contains contextual game design schemas that investigate the larger cultural contexts within which games are designed and played.

These schemas not only organize ways of looking at games — when taken as a whole, they offer a general method for the study of game design. Each schema brings certain aspects of games to light while building on previous schemas to arrive at a multivalent understanding. The three primary schemas are neither mutually exclusive nor scientific in nature. They are conceptual design tools to help focus thinking for particular design problems.

The framework extends beyond games. As a model for understanding any kind of design:

Rules
The organization of the designed system
Play
The human experience of that system
Culture
The larger contexts engaged with and inhabited by the system

In analyzing or creating a typeface, for example, one might study the formal rules of the system (how visual weights of the letterforms relate to each other), the play of the system (the kind of reading experience the typeface engenders), or the cultural aspects (historical references and the contexts where the typeface will be seen). Rules, Play, Culture is a structure that can facilitate critical design thinking in any design field.

Game Design Fundamentals

Rules of Play is a book about fundamentals. Game design has its own essential principles — a system of ideas that defines what games are and how they work. Innovation in the field can grow only from a deep understanding of these basic concepts. The game design fundamentals covered include:

Understanding design, systems, and interactivity, as well as player choice, action, and outcome. The study of rule-making and rule-breaking. Complexity and emergence. Game experience and game representation. Social game interaction. The powerful connection between the rules of a game and the play that the rules engender — the pleasures games invoke, the meanings they construct, the ideologies they embody, and the stories they tell.

As fundamental principles, these ideas form a system of building blocks that game designers arrange and rearrange in every game they create. As unlikely as it may sound, Go, Trivial Pursuit, Dance Dance Revolution, and Unreal Tournament all share the same fundamental principles, articulated in radically different ways. The range of game design expression is vast, deep, and largely unexplored.

The book is also very much about teaching and learning. Game design education represents an important counterpoint to game design theory and practice. The questions it forces us to ask are useful for any designer: What are the principle elements that constitute a game design curriculum? What does a student need to know to become a game designer? Rather than design a single curriculum, the book provides tools that allow educators to address their own particular circumstances — bibliography, suggested readings, case studies, commissioned games, and game design exercises, all built with flexibility in mind.

Further Readings

At the end of most chapters, the book includes a list of suggested further readings. These might be used to construct a syllabus for a class, a handout for a workshop, or to round out curiosity on a topic introduced in the chapter. The selected readings reflect the authors' own idiosyncrasies and are not meant as a definitive canon for game design theory — they represent what were felt to be the most relevant sources on each topic.

Further Reading — Chapter 1

The Art of Computer Game Design Chris Crawford

Written in 1982, at a time when computers were just beginning to appear in people's homes, this was one of the very first texts dealing with the nature of game design. Although some ideas have been dated by advances in the field, it remains an excellent resource for basic game design principles. Available online.

Gamasutra: The Art and Science of Making Games gamasutra.com

Part of the Gama Network. One of the best game design resources available — editorial features on practical game design problems, postmortems of commercial games, and academically-oriented writing on games and game design.

Game Studies: The International Journal of Computer Game Research Espen Aarseth, Markku Eskelinen, Marie-Laure Ryan, Susana Tosca (eds.) — gamestudies.org

A cross-disciplinary, peer-reviewed journal on computer and video games. Edited by academics and researchers with a deep interest in the study of games; the journal focuses on games research from a humanities and ludology perspective.

IGDA Curriculum Framework: The Study of Games and Game Development International Game Developers Association, Education Committee

A practical framework for a game design curriculum that addresses not only game design but related fields as diverse as visual design, programming, business, and humanities-based game studies. Modular in structure, applicable to a variety of educational contexts.

"I Have No Words but I Must Design" Greg Costikyan

Originally published in 1994 in Interactive Fantasy. An ambitious and influential essay attempting to formulate a critical vocabulary for game design, including a useful working definition of games. Short but consequential.

"Rules, Play, and Culture: Checkmate!" Frank Lantz & Eric Zimmerman

Originally published in 1999 in Merge Magazine. The first appearance of the three-part Rules/Play/Culture model for thinking about games, developed while the authors were teaching game design together at New York University. A brief, useful overview of the core game design topics that structure this book.
