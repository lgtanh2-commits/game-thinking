Games as the Play of Experience

How formal game systems transform into experiential ones through the core mechanic

Sudnow and the Breakout Experience

David Sudnow's memoir Pilgrim in the Microworld (1983) offers one of the most remarkable first-person accounts of game play ever written. Sudnow, a sociologist, became obsessed with Atari's Breakout — spending months learning to master the game through intensive practice. His account is not primarily about the game's rules but about the experience of learning to play: the way his hands, eyes, and mind gradually merged with the system.

What Sudnow describes is the transformation of a formal rule system into an experiential event — the way the rigid structure of Breakout became something felt, embodied, intuited.

This transformation — from formal to experiential — is exactly what this schema addresses. A game is more than its rules. It is an experience that players inhabit. Game designers are, ultimately, designers of experience.

Three Meanings of Experience

The word "experience" carries multiple meanings, each relevant to understanding games:

Apprehension The direct perception and apprehension of the world through the senses. Experience as the raw data of sensation — what you see, hear, feel during play.
Active Participation Experience as engagement — participating in an activity, taking action, making choices. The experience of doing, not merely perceiving.
Events Lived Through The cumulative, narrative sense of experience — events that leave a trace, that become part of who you are. "I have experience" in this sense means accumulated history.

All three meanings are at work when a player plays a game. They perceive the game world (apprehension), make choices and take actions (active participation), and accumulate a history of play that shapes future encounters with the game (events lived through).

Sutton-Smith's Psychological Model

Psychologist Brian Sutton-Smith identified five elements that describe the psychological processes by which players experience games — particularly video games:

Visual Scanning The rapid assessment of the game state through sight — tracking moving elements, reading the interface, building a spatial model of the play space.
Auditory Discriminations The parsing of game audio — distinguishing meaningful sounds (enemy approaching, reward collected) from background.
Motor Responses The physical actions players perform in response to what they perceive — button presses, joystick movements, the embodied side of play.
Concentration The focused attention games demand — narrowing awareness onto the game state, blocking out extraneous information.
Perceptual Patterns of Learning The gradual internalization of the game's patterns — learning to recognize enemy behaviors, predict outcomes, read the visual language of the game world.

Game design is a second-order design problem. The designer cannot directly design the player's experience — they can only design the formal system that, when set in motion by a player, produces experience. This indirection is both the challenge and the art of game design. Creating meaningful experiences means understanding the ways a game's formal system transforms into an experiential one.

The Core Mechanic

The most important concept in this schema is the core mechanic — the essential, moment-to-moment activity that players perform over and over during the course of a game. The core mechanic is the experiential heart of a game: the repeated action that creates larger patterns of play.

Tag The core mechanic is chasing and being chased — the physical act of running in pursuit or flight.
Verbal Tennis The core mechanic is responding to a question with a question — a dialogic back-and-forth of escalating improvisation.
LOOP The core mechanic is performing a looping butterfly pattern — a movement form that structures all other play.

A core mechanic can be a single activity, such as running in a footrace. A core mechanic can also be a compound activity, such as the military tactics, resource management, and mouse-and-keyboard skills of a real-time strategy game. Too often, game designers do not consider a game design on the level of the core mechanic, instead relying on conventional interactivity to determine the key player activity.

Case Study: Breakout and Its Variations

The Simple Core Mechanic

Breakout's core mechanic is strikingly simple: use a paddle to bounce a ball into a field of bricks. Hit a brick and it disappears. Clear all the bricks to advance. This simplicity is not a limitation but a strength — it creates an accessible, repeatable activity that can generate rich experiential variation.

Sudnow's obsession with Breakout is evidence of just how much experiential depth a simple core mechanic can contain. Within that single repeated action — paddle, ball, brick — lies a world of developing skill, pattern recognition, and physical feedback.

Atari 2600 Variations

Atari exploited the core mechanic's flexibility by releasing twelve variations of Breakout on the 2600. Each variation modified a single element while keeping the essential mechanic intact:

Timed Play within a time limit — urgency added to the mechanic.
Breakthru Ball passes through bricks rather than bouncing — new movement patterns.
Steering Player can influence ball direction mid-flight — expanded agency.
Catching Paddle can catch and hold the ball — introducing a new micro-action.
Invisible Bricks Bricks are hidden until hit — spatial memory becomes critical.
…and 7 more Each variant demonstrates a different dimension of the core mechanic's potential.

Alleyway (Game Boy)

Nintendo's Alleyway took the Breakout concept and added layers that deepened the experiential architecture. Open-sided walls allowed the ball to escape the play area, creating spatial anxiety. Multiple levels offered progressive challenge. Audio feedback — a distinctive crack when the ball broke through — gave players an acoustic reward that reinforced success.

These additions don't change the core mechanic but enrich the experience it generates. Each layer adds another dimension to what the player feels, hears, and must track.

Case Study: Centipede

Game designer Richard Rouse III offers an analysis of Centipede that illustrates how a game's five interdependent elements create escalating patterns of tension and release.

Mushrooms Clutter the play field, deflecting the centipede's descent and fragmenting into obstacles.
Centipedes The primary threat — segmented creatures descending toward the player, splitting when hit.
Fleas Drop mushrooms rapidly, cluttering the field. Arrive when the mushroom count gets too low.
Spiders Unpredictable movement that eats mushrooms. High-value targets but dangerous.
Scorpions Move across the field, poisoning mushrooms and causing centipedes to dive straight down — a devastating acceleration of threat.

These five elements are not independent — they form an interconnected system of escalating consequences. Shooting a centipede segment creates a mushroom. More mushrooms slow the centipede's descent. But they also attract fleas. Killing fleas depletes mushrooms, attracting more fleas. Scorpions poison what remains, forcing the centipede into a lethal dive.

Each level escalates this ecosystem of threat: more centipedes, faster movement, greater mushroom density. The entire game arc is built from these systemic relationships among five elements and their interactions — a masterclass in how a small number of well-designed components can create an experience that deepens over time.

Extending the Core Mechanic

The core mechanic can be extended and enlarged through the design of variations. Breakout provides a good example of a simple core mechanic that is intrinsically successful but which has been successfully modified into many variations. The lesson is not to find new core mechanics for every game — but to deeply understand the one you have, and explore its full range of experiential possibility.

What the core mechanic approach demands is that designers think first about the repeated lived activity at the heart of their game — not the story, not the setting, not the technology — but the action players will perform thousands of times during the course of play.

Chapter Summary

  • Play is experienced through participation. The formal game system is made manifest through the experiential engagement of a player.
  • Sutton-Smith's psychological model identifies five elements of game experience: concentration, visual scanning, auditory discriminations, motor responses, and perceptual patterns of learning.
  • Game experience follows a loop: game output → sensory input → internal decision → player action → game output.
  • Game design is a second-order design problem: designers indirectly design experience by directly designing formal systems.
  • The core mechanic is the essential moment-to-moment activity players enact — repeated over and over to create larger patterns of experience.
  • A core mechanic can be a single activity or a compound activity. It can be extended through variations, as Breakout demonstrates.
  • Centipede illustrates how a small number of interdependent elements can generate escalating, richly structured experiential patterns.
