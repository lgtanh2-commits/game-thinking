Breaking the Rules

Cheating, rule-bending, and the spoil-sport illuminate the edges of the magic circle — and reveal what game rules actually are.

The Spectrum of Players

How a player relates to the rules of a game is not a binary. Between the ideal player who follows every rule in perfect good faith and the spoil-sport who rejects the game entirely lies a wide spectrum of attitudes. Salen and Zimmerman propose five player types, defined along three axes: how strictly a player adheres to the rules, how intensely interested in winning they are, and how fully they have adopted what Bernard Suits calls the lusory attitude — the willingness to accept artificial obstacles for the sake of play.

Each type represents a different relationship to the game's social contract. The unsportsmanlike player — the boxer who repeatedly clinches to exhaust an opponent, or the basketball player who commits strategic fouls to stop the clock — operates entirely within operational rules while violating the implicit norms that give the game its character. They are, technically, not cheating. But they corrode the experience for everyone else.

The cheat occupies a peculiar philosophical position. By cheating, a player breaks the rules that define what winning means — and yet they still care deeply about winning. There is a paradox at the heart of cheating: the cheat accepts the game's quantifiable outcome as meaningful while rejecting the process that gives it meaning. Cheating is not spoiling; it is a corrupted form of play rather than the rejection of play.

The spoil-sport is, in many ways, more destructive than the cheat. The cheat works within the fiction of the magic circle while secretly violating it; the spoil-sport refuses the fiction entirely — knocking over the chess board, walking off the field, refusing to acknowledge the game's authority. This is why spoil-sports provoke such strong social reactions. They threaten not just one game but the entire idea of games.

Degenerate Strategies and Ecosystems

A degenerate strategy is a strategy that so dominates all others that it undermines the intended play of the game. When a degenerate strategy exists, it doesn't just make the game easier — it collapses the game's decision space. Players who use it are playing a different (much simpler) game. Players who don't are at a severe disadvantage. The meaningful choices the designer intended disappear.

Degenerate strategies are not always immediately obvious. They often emerge through the dedicated play of players who have mastered the rules and are pushing their limits. The tank rush in early versions of Command & Conquer — massing the cheapest unit and attacking before the opponent could build defenses — was a dominant strategy that many players found game-breaking. Over time, however, player communities developed counter-strategies, shifting the ecosystem until the tank rush was a known quantity rather than an automatic win. The game's community, in effect, patched a design problem that the designers had missed.

Rule changes driven by degenerate strategies are common in professional sports. Basketball's three-point line was introduced partly to open up floor spacing that dominant big men were collapsing; the three-second lane violation rule limits how long a player can camp in the paint. These rules did not just fix a problem — they changed what kinds of players were most valuable, reshaping the ecosystem of player types the game rewarded. A rule change is never just a rule change. It alters what the game is about.

Sanctioned Violations

Not all rule-breaking is treated equally, even within a single game. Professional sports have developed sophisticated systems for sanctioned violations — rule breaks that are officially recognized, penalized at a known cost, and accepted as part of play. These are not loopholes but formal acknowledgments that some violations are useful tactical moves worth the penalty.

A basketball player who commits a strategic foul late in the game is not cheating — they are making a rational calculation about the cost (two free throws) versus the benefit (stopping the clock, forcing pressure). The foul is sanctioned: it is in the rules as a defined violation with a defined consequence. The game has, in effect, priced the violation and made it available as a move.

Professional sports typically handle sanctioned violations on a sliding scale. Repeat offenders face escalating penalties — technical fouls accumulate toward ejection, yellow cards toward red cards, personal fouls toward disqualification. The system acknowledges that violations occur while giving referees authority to calibrate the response. Economic stakes amplify the effect: a player who commits a flagrant foul in a championship game faces reputational costs far beyond the technical penalty the rules impose.

Illuminati: Cheating as Mechanic

The card game Illuminati (Steve Jackson Games) provides an extraordinary case study in how cheating can be designed directly into a game's rules. The game is explicitly about conspiracy and deceit — and to match its theme, the designers chose to allow most forms of cheating within the game world, transforming what would normally be prohibited behavior into a sanctioned mechanic.

Illuminati Cheating Rules

Permitted (and thematically encouraged):

  • Lying about cards in hand
  • Making deals you have no intention of keeping
  • Concealing resources or plans from other players
  • Any form of deception, misdirection, or social manipulation

Forbidden (the three absolute prohibitions):

  • Tipping over the table
  • Using counterfeit money
  • Stealing from other players during the income phase

Punishment: If caught cheating in a forbidden way, the action is simply undone. No additional penalty. The boundary between sanctioned and unsanctioned cheating is sharp but the sanction is mild — the game is about conspiracy, and even catching a cheater is part of the play.

The Illuminati system works because the permitted cheating is thematically coherent with the game's subject matter — secret societies, manipulation, betrayal — and because the forbidden cheating represents acts that would destroy the game's physical or economic substrate rather than exploit it. Tipping the table is not a bold conspiratorial move; it is the act of a spoil-sport. Counterfeiting money undermines the game's resource system rather than using it cleverly. The line between cheating-as-move and cheating-as-destruction is drawn with precision.

Digital Rule-Breaking

Video games introduce new categories of rule-breaking unavailable in traditional games, because the operational rules are enforced by software rather than by social contract and referee authority. Some of these forms of rule-breaking are benign extensions of play; others are attacks on the game or its community.

  • Easter Eggs Secrets hidden by developers inside the game, invisible to the official rules but accessible through specific sequences of actions. The first Easter egg in a commercial video game appeared in Atari's Adventure (1979), where designer Warren Robinett secretly embedded his name in a hidden room — a personal credit in a corporate product that forbade individual attribution. Easter eggs are rule-breaks by designers, not players; they expand the game's possibility space in ways the official rules do not acknowledge.
  • Cheat Codes Sequences or inputs that activate designer-sanctioned alternate modes — invulnerability, infinite ammo, level select. Cheat codes are explicit sanctioned violations: the designers built them in, knowing they would be found and used. They bypass challenge without hiding the bypass. DOOM's cheat codes (IDDQD for god mode, IDKFA for full weapons) became cultural touchstones. Cheat codes are cheating-with-permission, transforming the game into a different kind of experience.
  • Walkthroughs External guides that provide complete solutions. Technically, using a walkthrough breaks no rules — the player still performs every required action. But walkthroughs defeat the game's information asymmetry: puzzles designed to produce uncertainty become mechanical execution tasks. They sit at the edge of rule-breaking — formally compliant, experientially transformative.
  • Workarounds Using the game's own systems in ways the designer did not intend, often to bypass designed obstacles. In Deus Ex, players discovered that proximity mines could be stacked against a wall and climbed like a ladder to reach areas otherwise inaccessible. This is not a bug exploit — the mine physics worked exactly as programmed — but a creative reinterpretation of the rules that produces an unintended affordance. Workarounds reveal the gap between operational rules and designed intent.
  • True Cheating Using external tools or exploits to gain advantages unavailable through the game's own rules — running multiple simultaneous accounts to manipulate outcomes, using bots to automate play, exploiting network packets. In SiSSYFiGHT 2000, players discovered they could open multiple browser sessions to control several characters simultaneously, gaining voting power far beyond what the social design intended. This crosses the line from workaround to genuine rule violation: it breaks the operational rules themselves.
  • Hacks Modifications to the game's code or data, ranging from benign (graphical mods, custom levels) to exploitative (aimbots, wallhacks) to destructive (server attacks). Raph Koster has noted that roughly half the engineering resources of a major massively multiplayer online game are devoted to anti-cheat systems — a measure of the design and economic cost that hacking imposes. The spoil-sport in digital form is the player who brings down a server, destroying the play experience for every other player in the game.

Rule-Breaking as Design Practice

The analysis of rule-breaking leads to a surprising conclusion: the most sophisticated players are, in some sense, game designers. When dedicated players find degenerate strategies, exploit workarounds, or discover Easter eggs, they are probing the edges of the designed system — discovering where the rules are inconsistent, incomplete, or produce unintended possibilities. This is exactly what game designers do when they test and iterate on their games.

Bernie DeKoven, in The Well-Played Game, extends this further. DeKoven argues that truly excellent play — play that is fully alive and meaningful — requires players to act as co-designers of the game. When a rule no longer serves the play, players should change it. When a configuration no longer produces the desired experience, players should redesign it. The game exists for the sake of play, not the other way around.

The well-played game is the product of people who understand games well enough to change them when they need to be changed. Players who have internalized the game so deeply that they can see through it to what the game is really trying to do — and adjust accordingly. — Bernie DeKoven, The Well-Played Game

For Salen and Zimmerman, this points toward the ultimate role of the game designer: a professional rule-breaker. The game designer's job is to construct a system that produces meaningful play — and to do that, they must constantly interrogate, revise, and break the rules they and others have established. The rules are a means to an end, not an end in themselves.

Chapter Summary

  • Players exist on a spectrum defined by three axes: adherence to operational rules, intensity of interest in winning, and depth of lusory attitude. The five types are standard player, dedicated player, unsportsmanlike player, cheat, and spoil-sport.
  • The cheat paradoxically respects the game's quantifiable outcome while violating the process that gives it meaning. The spoil-sport is more destructive: by rejecting the magic circle entirely, they threaten not just one game but the institution of play itself.
  • A degenerate strategy collapses the game's decision space by dominating all other strategies. Player communities sometimes patch degenerate strategies through emergent counter-play; rule changes address them formally — and always reshape the game's ecosystem of rewarded player types.
  • Sanctioned violations in professional sports price certain rule-breaks as tactical moves, with defined penalties and escalating consequences for repeat offenders.
  • Illuminati demonstrates how cheating can be designed as a mechanic: most deception is permitted and thematically coherent; only actions that would destroy the game's substrate are forbidden. Caught cheaters face the minimum consequence — undoing the action, no more.
  • Digital rule-breaking spans a spectrum from benign (Easter eggs, cheat codes, walkthroughs, workarounds) to damaging (true cheating, destructive hacks). Anti-cheat engineering consumes roughly half the resources of major MMO development — a measure of the design cost rule-breaking imposes at scale.
  • DeKoven's Well-Played Game argues that excellent players are co-designers of their games, empowered to change rules when rules no longer serve play. The game designer is, professionally, a rule-breaker: someone who constructs systems and then questions them in pursuit of meaningful play.
